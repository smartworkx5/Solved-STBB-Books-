BIEK Science General Part-I Result 2026 Explained: Figures, OMR and What Comes Next

By Solved Sindh Textbook Board Books Team · Updated 2026-10-30 · 1860 words · 9 min read

Science General is one of the largest groups in Karachi's intermediate system, and its HSC Part-I Annual Examination 2026 result arrived with the remaining groups on Monday, 26 October 2026, announced on the instructions of Board of Intermediate Education Karachi Chairman Faqir Muhammad Lakho, with Controller Zarina Rashid overseeing the release. The board's figures for the group are precise: 30,991 students registered, 30,386 appeared, and 15,036 passed all six papers. This guide explains what those figures mean for an individual student, how the science groups' E-assessment and OMR marking worked, what passing all six papers actually requires under the new 40% rule, and what students with open papers should do next. Results are available on the board's official website and its official Android app.

Science General Figures: Reading the Group Result

The three numbers for Science General tell a story that every student in the group should understand. Of 30,991 registered students, 30,386 appeared, which means about six hundred registered students did not sit the examination, and 15,036 of those who appeared passed all six papers. Put simply, just under half of the students who appeared cleared every paper, while just over half have at least one open paper, whether that means a single ungraded paper or several. That is not a reason for panic, because intermediate results routinely leave a large group with open papers, but it is a reason for honest self-assessment: if you have open papers, you are in the majority group, not an exception, and the board's formal processes exist precisely for your situation.

The comparison that matters most is not with other groups but with your own mark sheet. Science General students take six papers, and passing all six means clearing every one of them at or above the 40% threshold, because under the new grading system any paper below 40% is recorded as ungraded. A student who passed five papers and missed one by a few marks is in a very different position from a student who passed two papers, and the board's group figures cannot tell you which one you are. Read your own result paper by paper, list the passed and ungraded papers, and measure the gap below 40% for each open paper, because that list becomes your revision plan. E-Assessment and OMR: How Science Papers Were Marked

One distinctive feature of the science groups' assessment in 2026 was the use of E-Assessment through OMR sheets together with E-Marking for the science-group optional subjects. In plain terms, the objective parts of those papers were answered on machine-readable OMR sheets and marked electronically, which is designed to make marking faster and more consistent than hand-marking thousands of objective answers. For students, the practical meaning is that the objective section rewards careful sheet-filling as much as subject knowledge: a correctly known answer marked in the wrong bubble, or a half-filled bubble, can cost marks that knowledge alone would have earned, so students preparing for the next attempt should practise OMR-style answering as part of their revision.

E-Marking also changes how students should think about scrutiny and rechecking. Because the objective marking is electronic, a scrutiny request is more likely to be useful when there is a clear discrepancy, such as marks far below class performance or a suspected error in the subjective section, than when a student simply hopes for a few extra marks. The scrutiny fee reported from the board's scrutiny form is Rs 1,000 per paper, and no scrutiny deadline is stated in the material this guide is based on, so students should check the board's official website for the current notice. Whether or not you apply for scrutiny, revise the open paper in parallel, because waiting for a scrutiny outcome before opening the book wastes the most valuable resource you have, which is time.

What Passing All Six Papers Really Means

Passing all six papers sounds simple, but under the new grading system it has a precise meaning that students should understand. It means scoring at or above 40% in every one of the six papers, with no paper left ungraded, because a single paper below 40% breaks the full pass even if the other five are strong. This is why the 15,036 figure matters: those are the students who cleared every paper, while every other appeared student has at least one paper still open. Students sometimes add up their total marks and assume a high total compensates for a weak paper, but the system works paper by paper, and a strong performance in one subject does not rescue an ungraded paper in another.

For students who passed all six, the message is straightforward: congratulations, and carry the same standard into Part-II, where the same 40% logic applies. For students with open papers, the message is equally clear: your job is defined by the list of open papers, not by your total, and it should be tackled paper by paper, starting with the paper closest to 40%. Work from the textbook, practise objective questions in OMR style where relevant, and write subjective answers under timed conditions, because both halves of a science paper need practice. If You Have Open Papers: A Practical Plan

Begin by making the paper-by-paper list and sharing it with a parent and a teacher, because adults who see the exact gaps can help you plan realistically and can tell you what support your college offers, such as extra classes or remedial sessions. Then set a daily revision slot for the open papers alongside your Part-II study, protecting Part-II college hours while giving the open papers focused time each day. Start with the paper closest to passing so that an early success builds momentum, and use the overlap between Part-I and Part-II science chapters deliberately, since revising a Part-I concept often strengthens the Part-II chapter built on it. Keep a weekly checklist of chapters revised and practice tests completed, and review it every weekend.

Avoid the two most common mistakes. The first is trusting unofficial dates, fee claims or agents who promise shortcuts; the board's official website, www.biek.edu.pk, and your college notice board are the only reliable sources for reappearance and scrutiny notices, and no reappearance date should be treated as confirmed unless it appears there. The second is stopping study while waiting for a notice, which turns a small gap into a forgotten subject by the time the examination arrives. Keep your result, admit card and registration details safe, because the reappearance process will require them, and treat every claim about dates or fees that does not come from the board as unverified until you have checked it yourself.

Science General in Context: The Full 26 October Release

Science General was announced alongside Science Pre-Medical, Commerce in Regular and Private categories, Humanities and Arts in Regular and Private categories, Special Candidates in Arts Regular, and Home Economics, completing the HSC Part-I results after Pre-Engineering had already been announced on 2 October 2026. For comparison, Pre-Medical reported 25,157 registered, 24,544 appeared and 11,966 passing all six papers, while Commerce Regular reported 23,549 registered, 22,693 appeared and 9,332 passing all seven papers, and Special Candidates in Arts Regular reported 99 registered, 97 appeared and 58 passing all six. These comparisons are useful only in a general sense, because each group has different subjects and, in Commerce's case, a different number of papers, so students should not judge their own result by another group's pass rate.

What the full release does show is that the new grading system now runs across the intermediate groups, with the same 40% logic and the same ungraded outcome for any paper below that line. Science General students should therefore treat this year's result as the baseline for how the system works, not as an exception, and should build study habits that aim comfortably above 40% in every paper rather than at the borderline. That margin protects against a hard paper, an off day, or a strict marking scheme, and it carries directly into Part-II. Frequently asked questions

How many Science General students passed all six papers in 2026?

According to the board's release, 15,036 students passed all six papers out of 30,386 who appeared, from 30,991 registered. Just over half of the students who appeared therefore have at least one open paper, which is a normal outcome with a formal process for clearing it.

What is E-Assessment and OMR marking?

For the science-group optional subjects, objective answers were given on machine-readable OMR sheets and marked electronically, together with E-Marking. This makes objective marking faster and more consistent, and it means students should practise careful OMR sheet-filling alongside subject revision.

I passed five papers but one is ungraded. Have I passed Part-I?

No, not fully. Passing all six means clearing every paper at or above 40%, and a single ungraded paper breaks the full pass. List the open paper, measure its gap below 40%, revise it, and reappear subject to eligibility under the board's official notice.

Does a high total compensate for one weak paper?

No. The system works paper by paper, so a strong performance in one subject does not rescue an ungraded paper in another. Each paper must individually reach the 40% threshold to count as passed.

Where do I check official BIEK notices about scrutiny or reappearance?

On the board's official website, www.biek.edu.pk, and the official BIEK Android app, plus your college notice board. Treat dates, fees or procedures from any other source as unverified until you have confirmed them officially.
