Maths Word Problems: Money and Shopping

By Solved Sindh Textbook Board Books Team · Updated 2026-11-04 · 1914 words · 10 min read

Money word problems feel easy because we all go shopping, yet many students lose marks on them in exams. The reason is rarely the calculation itself; it is reading the problem too fast and mixing up what is given and what is asked. A money problem usually hides a simple addition, subtraction, multiplication or division inside a small story about buying, paying, saving or getting change. If you slow down, underline the numbers, name what each number means, and decide the operation before touching your pencil, the story turns into a short calculation. This guide teaches you a steady method for change problems, discount problems and total bill problems, with worked examples you can copy into your practice notebook. You can also check our [solved notes](/solved.html) and the [books page](/books.html) for more practice material.

Read the Problem Like a Shopkeeper

Before you calculate anything, read the whole problem twice and ask yourself three questions: what did the person buy, how much money did they have or pay, and what exactly is the question asking for. Many students start adding numbers the moment they see them, but a money problem often gives you one extra number you do not need, such as the number of shops visited or the colour of a bag. Circle only the numbers that belong to the question, and write a short note beside each one, for example price of one notebook, number of notebooks, or money given to the shopkeeper. This small habit prevents the most common error in money problems, which is using the right numbers in the wrong operation.

Next, decide the operation by thinking about the story, not by guessing. If things are being bought together, you add. If money is being returned, you subtract. If the same item is bought several times, you multiply. If a total amount is shared or split, you divide. Write the operation in words first, such as total cost equals price times quantity, and only then put in the numbers. Students who write the plan first almost never mix up addition and multiplication, because the plan forces them to think about what is happening in the story. Practice this on five problems a day, and the method will soon feel automatic in exams.

Change Problems: What You Get Back

A change problem always follows the same two steps: first find the total cost of everything bought, then subtract that total from the money paid. Suppose Sara buys a pencil for 25 rupees and an eraser for 15 rupees, and she pays with a 100-rupee note. The total cost is 25 plus 15, which is 40 rupees. The change is 100 minus 40, which is 60 rupees. The most common mistake here is subtracting only one item from the money paid and forgetting the other, so always add all the items first and subtract once at the end. Writing the two steps on separate lines makes your working clear and earns full marks even if a small slip happens later.

Change problems get trickier when quantities appear, because you must multiply before you subtract. For example, if Ahmed buys 3 pens at 30 rupees each and pays with a 200-rupee note, the total cost is 3 times 30, which is 90 rupees, and the change is 200 minus 90, which is 110 rupees. Notice the order: multiplication first, then subtraction. A useful check is to add the change back to the total cost; the result must equal the money paid. In this case 110 plus 90 equals 200, so the answer is correct. This reverse check takes five seconds and catches most careless errors before you move on.

Discount Problems: Less Than the Price Tag

A discount means the shop reduces the price, so the customer pays less than the marked price. To solve a discount problem, first find the discount amount and then subtract it from the marked price. For example, a shirt marked at 800 rupees is sold at a discount of 100 rupees. The selling price is 800 minus 100, which is 700 rupees. Some problems give the discount as a percentage instead, such as 10 percent off. Ten percent of 800 is 80 rupees, so the selling price is 800 minus 80, which is 720 rupees. Always check whether the problem gives the discount in rupees or in percent, because the method changes completely.

A classic exam trap is asking for the discount amount when only the two prices are given. If a bag marked at 500 rupees is sold for 450 rupees, the discount is 500 minus 450, which is 50 rupees. Another trap is giving the selling price and the discount and asking for the marked price; here you must add, not subtract, because the marked price is higher than the selling price. For instance, if a toy is sold for 360 rupees after a discount of 40 rupees, its marked price was 360 plus 40, which is 400 rupees. Drawing a tiny number line with marked price, discount and selling price helps you see which operation to use.

Total Bill Problems: Adding Items and Quantities

A total bill problem asks you to combine several purchases into one final amount, and it tests whether you can handle both addition and multiplication in the same question. The safe method is to make a small table with three columns: item, price of one, and quantity. Multiply across each row to get the cost of that item, then add the rows down to get the bill. For example, 2 notebooks at 60 rupees each cost 120 rupees, and 4 pencils at 10 rupees each cost 40 rupees, so the total bill is 120 plus 40, which is 160 rupees. A table keeps your working tidy and lets your teacher follow every step.

Watch out for problems that mix units, such as buying items in dozens or paying partly in cash and partly from savings. If eggs cost 20 rupees each and a dozen are bought, the quantity is 12, not 1, so the cost is 12 times 20, which is 240 rupees. If a bill of 850 rupees is paid with 500 rupees in cash and the rest from a wallet, the wallet amount is 850 minus 500, which is 350 rupees. Read every quantity word carefully, especially dozen, pair, packet and kilogram, because these words change the number you multiply by. After finishing, reread the question once to confirm you answered exactly what was asked.

Common Mistakes in Money Problems

The first common mistake is rushing the reading and using a number that the question never asked about, such as the age of the buyer or the number of shops. The second is forgetting to multiply when several identical items are bought, so a student adds the price once instead of three times. The third is subtracting in the wrong order in change problems, for example subtracting the money paid from the total cost, which gives a negative or impossible answer. The fourth is confusing discount with selling price, writing the discount amount as the final answer when the question asked what the customer actually paid. Each of these mistakes is avoidable with the slow, step-by-step method taught above.

Another frequent error is losing track of rupees and paisas, or mixing up the decimal point when prices include paisas, such as 45.50 rupees. Always line up decimal points in addition and subtraction, and treat 45.50 as 45 rupees and 50 paisas. Finally, never leave a money answer without its unit: write 60 rupees, not just 60. In exams, missing units and missing working both cost marks even when the final number is right. Build a habit of showing every step, labelling every amount, and doing the reverse check on change problems, and money word problems will become one of your most reliable sources of marks. 5 key points you must know

  • Read every money problem twice and underline only the numbers the question needs.
  • Change equals money paid minus total cost; always add all items before subtracting.
  • Multiply price by quantity before adding items into a total bill.
  • A discount in rupees is subtracted directly; a percent discount must be calculated first.
  • Check change problems backwards: change plus total cost must equal the money paid.

Frequently asked questions

What is the first step in solving a money word problem?

The first step is reading the whole problem twice and identifying what is bought, what each item costs, how much money is paid, and what the question actually asks. Many students fail because they start calculating before understanding the story. Write down each number with a short label, such as price of one pen or money given, and cross out any extra number the question does not need. This careful start makes the rest of the problem much easier.

How do I calculate change in a shopping problem?

First find the total cost of everything bought, multiplying price by quantity where needed, and then subtract that total from the money paid. For example, if items cost 140 rupees in total and you pay with a 500-rupee note, the change is 500 minus 140, which is 360 rupees. Always check your answer by adding the change back to the total cost; the result must equal the money paid.

What is the difference between discount and selling price?

The discount is the amount reduced from the marked price, while the selling price is what the customer actually pays after that reduction. If a shirt is marked at 800 rupees with a discount of 100 rupees, the selling price is 700 rupees. Students often write the discount amount as the final answer by mistake, so always reread the question to see whether it asks for the discount, the selling price, or the marked price.

How do I solve a total bill problem with several items?

Make a small table with item, price of one, and quantity, multiply across each row to find each item's cost, and then add all the rows to get the total bill. This method keeps your working organised and prevents the common error of adding a price once when several identical items were bought. After finding the total, reread the question to confirm you answered exactly what was asked, such as the bill or the change.

Why do I keep making mistakes in money problems?

Most mistakes come from rushing the reading, forgetting to multiply for quantities, subtracting in the wrong order, or confusing discount with selling price. Slow down, write a plan in words before calculating, show every step on separate lines, and use the reverse check for change problems. Practising five problems a day with this method will quickly turn money problems into easy marks in your exams.
