Maths Word Problems: Fractions of Pizza and Pocket Money

By Solved Sindh Textbook Board Books Team · Updated 2026-11-04 · 1724 words · 9 min read

Fractions describe parts of a whole, and the best way to understand them is through food and money: slices of a pizza, pieces of a chocolate bar, or parts of your pocket money spent and saved. Fraction word problems test whether you can find a fraction of an amount, add or subtract fractions, and handle the tricky case where a fraction is taken of what remains rather than of the original whole. Students lose marks mainly by applying a fraction to the wrong whole, so this guide teaches you to always ask: fraction of what. Worked pizza and pocket money examples make each method concrete. More practice is in our [solved notes](/solved.html) and on the [books page](/books.html).

A Fraction Is Part of a Whole

A fraction has two parts: the denominator tells you how many equal parts the whole is divided into, and the numerator tells you how many of those parts you have. In the fraction three quarters, the whole is divided into 4 equal parts and you have 3 of them. To find a fraction of an amount, divide the amount by the denominator and multiply by the numerator. For example, three quarters of 80 rupees is 80 divided by 4, which is 20, times 3, which is 60 rupees. This divide-then-multiply method works for every fraction-of-an-amount problem, whatever the numbers.

It helps to visualise fractions with a simple bar or circle divided into equal parts, shading the parts the question mentions. Drawing takes only seconds and prevents the most common error, which is swapping the numerator and denominator. If a problem says a pizza is cut into 8 slices and Ahmed eats 3, the fraction eaten is 3 over 8, and the fraction remaining is 5 over 8, because 8 minus 3 equals 5 slices left. Always check that the fractions for eaten and remaining add up to the whole, which is 1, because 3 over 8 plus 5 over 8 equals 8 over 8, which is 1.

Pizza Problems: Sharing Food Fairly

Pizza problems usually ask how much each person gets when a pizza is shared, or how much remains after some slices are eaten. If 3 pizzas are shared equally among 4 friends, each friend gets 3 over 4 of a pizza, because the total pizzas divided by the number of friends gives the share. If one pizza is cut into 12 slices and Sara eats 5 slices, she has eaten 5 over 12 of the pizza, and 7 over 12 remains. When slices are different sizes, the fraction only works if the parts are equal, so exam questions always state that the pizza is cut equally.

A harder pizza problem combines two fractions, such as eating one half of a pizza and then one quarter of what remains. After eating one half, one half remains, and one quarter of that remaining half is one eighth of the whole pizza, leaving three eighths. The trap is to add one half and one quarter to get three quarters eaten, which is wrong, because the quarter applies to the remaining half, not to the whole pizza. Always identify the whole for each fraction separately, and draw the pizza in stages if the problem has two steps.

Pocket Money Problems: Spending and Saving

Pocket money problems apply fractions to money, and they test the same divide-then-multiply method. If Bilal receives 200 rupees and spends two fifths of it, he spends 200 divided by 5, which is 40, times 2, which is 80 rupees, leaving 120 rupees. If he then saves one half of what remains, he saves 60 rupees, because the second fraction applies to the remaining 120, not to the original 200. This two-step structure is the most tested pattern in fraction problems, and the phrase of what remains is the signal that the whole has changed.

Another common type gives the fraction spent and the amount left, and asks for the original pocket money. If Sara spends three quarters of her money and has 50 rupees left, the remaining fraction is one quarter, so one quarter equals 50 rupees, and the whole amount is 4 times 50, which is 200 rupees. Working backwards like this requires finding the remaining fraction first, which many students forget. Always write down both the spent fraction and the remaining fraction before calculating, because the remaining fraction is often the key to the whole problem.

Fractions of a Remaining Amount

Fractions of a remaining amount confuse students because the whole changes halfway through the problem, and each fraction must be applied to the correct whole. Consider a ribbon 24 metres long: first one third is cut off, leaving 16 metres, and then one quarter of the remainder is cut off, which is 4 metres, leaving 12 metres. The second fraction, one quarter, applies to 16 metres, not to 24 metres. If you mistakenly apply it to 24, you get 6 metres and a wrong final answer of 10 metres instead of 12. Writing the remaining amount after each step prevents this error completely.

A useful check is to track the remaining fraction as well as the remaining amount. After cutting one third, two thirds remain. After cutting one quarter of the remainder, three quarters of the remainder survive, which is three quarters of two thirds, or one half of the original. One half of 24 metres is 12 metres, confirming the step-by-step answer. Multiplying fractions like this is an advanced check, but even without it, the habit of rewriting the remaining amount after every step will carry you through the hardest fraction problems in your exams.

Common Mistakes in Fraction Problems

The first common mistake is applying a fraction to the original whole when the question says of the remainder, which changes the answer completely. The second is swapping the numerator and denominator, for example using 4 over 3 instead of 3 over 4. The third is adding fractions with different denominators without finding a common denominator, such as claiming one half plus one third equals two fifths. The fourth is forgetting that the parts must be equal for a fraction to make sense, which matters when a story mentions unequal slices. The fifth is leaving an answer as an improper fraction when the question expects a mixed number, or vice versa.

To avoid these errors, always start by naming the whole, draw a quick bar showing the parts, and write the remaining amount after each step in a multi-step problem. Simplify fractions only at the end unless the question asks for simplest form, and label your final answer with its unit, such as slices or rupees. With steady practice on pizza and pocket money stories, fractions stop feeling abstract and become a reliable source of marks. More guides are on our blog and about page.

5 key points you must know

  • To find a fraction of an amount, divide by the denominator and multiply by the numerator.
  • Always ask: fraction of what — the original whole or the remaining amount.
  • After each step in a multi-step problem, rewrite the remaining amount before continuing.
  • Fractions for eaten and remaining parts must add up to the whole, which is 1.
  • Draw a quick bar or circle divided into equal parts to visualise any fraction problem.

Frequently asked questions

How do I find a fraction of a number?

Divide the number by the denominator and multiply the result by the numerator. For example, three quarters of 80 is 80 divided by 4, which is 20, times 3, which is 60. This divide-then-multiply method works for any fraction and any amount, including money, lengths and quantities of food. Drawing a bar divided into equal parts helps you see why the method works and prevents swapping the numerator and denominator.

What does fraction of the remainder mean?

It means the fraction applies to whatever is left after an earlier step, not to the original whole. If you spend one half of 200 rupees, 100 rupees remain, and spending one quarter of the remainder means spending 25 rupees, not 50. The phrase of what remains is the signal that the whole has changed, so always rewrite the remaining amount after each step before applying the next fraction.

How do I share pizzas equally among friends?

Divide the total number of pizzas by the number of friends to find each share as a fraction. If 3 pizzas are shared among 4 friends, each friend gets 3 over 4 of a pizza. If a single pizza is cut into 12 equal slices and someone eats 5, they have eaten 5 over 12 of the pizza and 7 over 12 remains. The parts must be equal for the fraction to be valid, so exam questions always state that the pizza is cut equally.

How do I work backwards from a remaining amount?

First find the remaining fraction by subtracting the spent fraction from 1, then use the remaining amount to find the whole. If Sara spends three quarters of her money and has 50 rupees left, the remaining fraction is one quarter, so one quarter equals 50 rupees and the whole amount is 200 rupees. Writing down both the spent and remaining fractions before calculating is the key step students most often skip.

Why do fraction word problems go wrong?

Most errors come from applying a fraction to the wrong whole, especially in of the remainder problems, swapping numerator and denominator, or adding fractions with different denominators without a common denominator. Drawing a bar diagram, naming the whole for each fraction, rewriting the remaining amount after each step, and checking that parts add up to the whole will prevent nearly all of these mistakes.
