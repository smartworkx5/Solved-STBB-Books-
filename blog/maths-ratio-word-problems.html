Maths Word Problems: Ratio in Recipes and Classes

By Solved Sindh Textbook Board Books Team · Updated 2026-11-04 · 1887 words · 9 min read

Ratio problems sound difficult, but they describe something you already do every day: sharing food fairly, mixing ingredients in a recipe, or comparing the number of boys and girls in a class. A ratio simply compares two or more quantities of the same kind, and the most important skill is splitting a total into parts according to that ratio. Once you learn the total-parts method, sharing problems, recipe problems and class problems all follow the same pattern. This guide explains ratios from the basics, works through sharing and mixing examples, and shows you the mistakes that cost marks. You can practise further with our [solved notes](/solved.html) and the [books page](/books.html).

What a Ratio Really Means

A ratio like 2:3 means that for every 2 parts of one thing, there are 3 parts of another thing, making 5 parts in total. The numbers in a ratio are not the actual amounts; they are the shares. If 2:3 describes the boys and girls in a group of 25 students, the total number of parts is 5, so each part is worth 25 divided by 5, which is 5 students. That gives 2 times 5, which is 10 boys, and 3 times 5, which is 15 girls. Notice that 10 plus 15 equals 25, which confirms the answer. This total-parts method is the key to nearly every ratio problem you will meet.

Ratios must compare quantities of the same kind and in the same units, and they should be written in simplest form. The ratio 10:15 simplifies to 2:3 by dividing both numbers by 5, just like simplifying a fraction. A common exam question gives you a ratio and one actual amount and asks for the other amount. For example, if the ratio of red to blue pens is 3:4 and there are 12 red pens, then one part is worth 12 divided by 3, which is 4 pens, so the blue pens number 4 times 4, which is 16. Always find the value of one part first; everything else follows from it.

Sharing Problems: Dividing a Total Fairly

Sharing problems give you a total amount and a ratio, and ask how much each person or group receives. The method never changes: add the ratio numbers to find the total parts, divide the total amount by the total parts to find one part, then multiply by each share. For example, a prize of 500 rupees is shared between two friends in the ratio 3:2. The total parts are 5, so one part is worth 500 divided by 5, which is 100 rupees. The first friend receives 3 times 100, which is 300 rupees, and the second receives 2 times 100, which is 200 rupees. Add the shares back together to check: 300 plus 200 equals 500.

Sharing problems sometimes ask for the difference between two shares instead of the shares themselves. If 720 rupees is shared in the ratio 5:3, the total parts are 8, so one part is worth 90 rupees. The shares are 450 and 270 rupees, and the difference is 450 minus 270, which is 180 rupees. A shortcut is to subtract the ratio numbers first, 5 minus 3 equals 2 parts, and multiply by one part, 2 times 90 equals 180 rupees. Both routes give the same answer, but the shortcut only works once you have found the value of one part. In exams, show the full working so partial marks are available even if the last step slips.

Mixing Problems: Recipes and Paint

Recipe problems use ratios to scale ingredients up or down, and they test whether you can keep the proportions correct. Suppose a recipe for 4 people uses flour and sugar in the ratio 3:1, with 300 grams of flour and 100 grams of sugar. For 8 people, you double everything, so you need 600 grams of flour and 200 grams of sugar, and the ratio stays 3:1. The trick is that scaling a recipe never changes the ratio; it only changes the actual amounts. If a problem asks how much sugar is needed when 450 grams of flour are used, note that one part is 150 grams, because 3 parts equal 450, so the sugar is 1 part, which is 150 grams.

Paint and mixture problems work exactly the same way. If blue and white paint are mixed in the ratio 2:5 to make 35 litres of paint, the total parts are 7, so one part is 5 litres. The mixture contains 2 times 5, which is 10 litres of blue paint, and 5 times 5, which is 25 litres of white paint. A common trap is giving the amount of only one ingredient and asking for the total mixture. If a mixture contains 10 litres of blue paint in the ratio 2:5, one part is 5 litres, so the total mixture is 7 times 5, which is 35 litres. Always identify whether the given amount is one share, one part, or the total.

Ratios in a Class: Boys, Girls and Groups

Class problems often give the ratio of boys to girls and the total number of students, or the ratio and the difference between the numbers. For a class of 40 students with a boy-to-girl ratio of 3:5, the total parts are 8, so one part is worth 5 students. That gives 15 boys and 25 girls, and 15 plus 25 equals 40, which checks out. If instead the problem says there are 10 more girls than boys with a ratio of 3:5, the difference in parts is 2, so one part is worth 10 divided by 2, which is 5 students, giving the same 15 boys and 25 girls. The difference method is just the total-parts method applied to a difference.

Some class problems combine ratios with fractions or percentages, so keep the ideas separate in your working. If the ratio of boys to girls is 3:5, the fraction of the class that is boys is 3 out of 8, and the percentage is 37.5 percent. You do not need percentages to solve a ratio problem, but converting between ratio, fraction and percentage is a favourite exam question. Practise by taking one ratio, such as 2:3, and writing it as a fraction of each part, as a simplified ratio, and as percentages. This flexibility turns ratio questions from a feared topic into an easy source of marks.

Common Mistakes in Ratio Problems

The most common mistake is treating the ratio numbers as the actual amounts, for example writing that a 2:3 ratio means 2 boys and 3 girls when the class actually has 25 students. The second mistake is forgetting to add the ratio numbers to find total parts, and instead dividing the total by only one of the numbers. The third is mixing up the order of the ratio, giving the larger share to the wrong person, which matters when the problem names who gets which share. The fourth is failing to simplify a ratio when the question asks for the simplest form, leaving an answer like 10:15 instead of 2:3.

Another error is adding ratios together as if they were fractions, or adding a ratio to a total that includes something else, such as counting the teacher in a class ratio meant only for students. Always check that the total you divide matches what the ratio describes. Finally, label your shares clearly: write boys equals 15 and girls equals 25, not just two bare numbers. Clear labels make your answer easy to mark and easy to check. With the total-parts method practised daily, ratio problems become some of the quickest marks in the paper. 5 key points you must know

  • A ratio compares shares, not actual amounts; always find the value of one part first.
  • Sharing method: add ratio numbers for total parts, divide the total, then multiply by each share.
  • Scaling a recipe changes amounts but never changes the ratio itself.
  • For difference problems, divide the difference by the difference in parts to find one part.
  • Check every ratio answer by adding the shares back to the original total.

Frequently asked questions

What is a ratio in simple words?

A ratio compares two or more quantities of the same kind by showing how many parts of each there are. For example, a ratio of 2:3 means that for every 2 parts of one thing there are 3 parts of another, making 5 parts in total. The numbers in a ratio are shares, not actual amounts, so to find real amounts you must first work out what one part is worth by dividing the total by the total number of parts.

How do I share an amount in a given ratio?

Add the ratio numbers to get the total parts, divide the total amount by the total parts to find the value of one part, and then multiply one part by each share in the ratio. For example, sharing 500 rupees in the ratio 3:2 gives total parts of 5, one part of 100 rupees, and shares of 300 and 200 rupees. Always check by adding the shares together; they must equal the original total.

How do ratio problems work in recipes?

A recipe ratio shows the proportions of ingredients, and scaling the recipe up or down multiplies every ingredient by the same factor without changing the ratio. If a recipe uses flour and sugar in the ratio 3:1 with 300 grams of flour, then using 450 grams of flour means one part is 150 grams, so the sugar needed is 150 grams. The key skill is finding the value of one part from whichever ingredient amount you are given.

What if a ratio problem gives the difference, not the total?

When a problem gives the difference between two shares, subtract the ratio numbers to find the difference in parts, then divide the given difference by that number to find one part. For example, if girls exceed boys by 10 in a ratio of 3:5, the difference in parts is 2, so one part is 5, giving 15 boys and 25 girls. Once you know one part, every other value follows by multiplication.

Why do students get ratio questions wrong?

The most common errors are treating ratio numbers as actual amounts, forgetting to add the ratio numbers for total parts, mixing up the order of the ratio, and failing to simplify the final ratio. Students also sometimes divide a total that does not match what the ratio describes, such as including the teacher in a student-only ratio. Labelling each share clearly and checking that shares add back to the total prevents nearly all of these mistakes.
