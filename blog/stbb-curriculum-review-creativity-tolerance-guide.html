STBB Curriculum Review: Creativity, Critical Thinking and Tolerance in School Books

By Solved Sindh Textbook Board Books Team · Updated 2026-11-03 · 1459 words · 7 min read

School textbooks shape how millions of students think, and a recent meeting of the Sindh Textbook Board's curriculum review committee has put three qualities at the centre of that shaping: creativity, critical thinking and tolerance. The committee, chaired by STBB Chairman Parvez Ahmed Baloch and held on the directives of Sindh Minister for Education Syed Sardar Ali Shah, reviewed the 12th-grade Sindhi textbook, heard experts' observations, and recommended a comprehensive review of all STBB publications. This article explains what the review means, why those three qualities matter, and what students and teachers can expect as the revised curriculum arrives from the next academic year.

The Meeting: Who Met, What Was Reviewed, What Was Recommended

The first meeting of the STBB curriculum review committee was held to evaluate educational textbooks, on the directives of Sindh Minister for Education Syed Sardar Ali Shah, and was chaired by STBB Chairman Parvez Ahmed Baloch. The committee reviewed the 12th-grade Sindhi textbook, and experts shared their observations and suggested revisions in line with contemporary educational needs. That choice of starting point matters: a 12th-grade language textbook is read by students at the threshold of adult life, and the values and thinking habits it models travel with them beyond school.

Two recommendations came out of the meeting. First, the committee emphasised the inclusion of critical thinking, creativity and tolerance in school curricula. Second, it recommended a comprehensive review of all STBB publications, not just the single textbook examined at this meeting. Together, those two points signal a programme rather than a one-off edit: every STBB book is to be examined against the same standards. This article describes the meeting as recent, because its report gives no exact date, and no date is invented for it here.

The Scale Behind the Review: 195 Titles and 52 Million Sets

To understand why a curriculum review matters so much, consider the scale of STBB publishing. The STBB 2026-27 textbooks, published under the Shaheed Mohtarma Benazir Bhutto Textbook Series from Early Childhood to Grade 10, comprise 195 titles in Sindhi, Urdu and English, with 52 million sets, and distribution was inaugurated on 19 February 2026. A change to the approach of these books therefore reaches an enormous number of classrooms, and a review that touches all publications is one of the largest educational undertakings in the province.

Scale also explains why the revised curriculum is expected from the next academic year rather than immediately. Textbooks must be reviewed, revised, approved, printed and distributed, and that cycle cannot be rushed without risking errors. Students currently studying from existing books should therefore continue with their current textbooks and syllabi, since those remain the basis for current examinations. The review is a direction of travel for the books of the coming years, not a change to the paper a student will sit next week.

Creativity: More Than Art Lessons

Creativity in a curriculum does not mean only drawing or art classes. It means textbooks that invite students to imagine alternatives, to write their own endings to a story, to design their own solutions to a problem, and to ask what else could be true. A science chapter that asks students to design a simple experiment practises creativity. A language lesson that asks for an original paragraph practises creativity. A social studies question that asks how a community problem could be solved in three different ways practises creativity.

For students, the practical way to build creativity now is to go beyond memorising the textbook answer. After finishing a lesson, try one extra task of your own: write a different ending, propose a different solution, or explain the idea to a classmate in your own words. Teachers can support this by rewarding original, well-reasoned answers even when they differ from the model answer, and by setting at least one open-ended question in class tests. Creativity grows when it is practised and praised, not when it is only mentioned in a policy document.

Critical Thinking and Tolerance: Thinking Clearly, Living Together

Critical thinking means examining a claim before accepting it: asking what evidence supports it, what evidence counts against it, and whether a different explanation fits better. In school, it is practised whenever a student compares two accounts of an event, checks a statement against a source, or explains why an argument is weak. Textbooks that include questions asking students to evaluate, compare and judge, rather than only recall, are building this skill. Students can practise it today by asking, for every important claim in a lesson, what reason the book gives and whether that reason is strong.

Tolerance, in a curriculum, means learning to live respectfully with people who differ in religion, language, culture or opinion. The committee's emphasis on tolerance sits alongside a related verified development: Sindh Hindu religious textbooks have been approved for Hindu students in government schools, in Grades III to V, for the academic year 2026-27, following a Sindh Curriculum Council Executive Committee meeting on 20 April 2026 and a letter to the STBB chairman on 29 April 2026. Textbooks that present diversity fairly, and classrooms where different views are discussed respectfully, teach tolerance far more effectively than a single lesson about it. What Students and Teachers Should Do Now

Students should not wait for new books to start thinking creatively, critically and tolerantly. The habits can begin with current textbooks: question claims, propose alternatives, discuss respectfully, and write original answers. When revised books arrive from the next academic year, students who already practise these habits will find the new material natural rather than strange. Parents can help by encouraging discussion at home, by asking children what they think about a lesson rather than only what they memorised, and by modelling respectful disagreement.

Teachers are the bridge between a review committee's recommendations and a classroom's reality. A teacher who sets one open question per lesson, who asks students to justify their answers, and who ensures every student is heard respectfully is already delivering the curriculum the committee described, whatever edition of the textbook is on the desk. The comprehensive review of all STBB publications will take time, and its success will ultimately be measured not by committee minutes but by whether classrooms feel more curious, more thoughtful and more respectful. That is a goal worth working toward starting today.
