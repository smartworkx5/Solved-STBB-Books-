Idioms and Phrasal Verbs for Daily English

By Solved Sindh Textbook Board Books Team · Updated 2026-10-18 · 1167 words · 6 min read

Native speakers use phrasal verbs and idioms constantly, often without noticing. The idioms phrasal verbs daily English pupils need most are not rare or literary; they are the everyday expressions for starting, stopping, continuing, giving up, and getting along. This guide introduces twenty high-frequency phrasal verbs with short dialogues you can reuse, explains how phrasal verbs are built, and shows how to use idioms safely without sounding forced. You will learn the difference between separable and inseparable phrasal verbs, why literal translation fails, and how to practise a few expressions at a time so they stay in your active vocabulary.

What Phrasal Verbs Are and How They Work

A phrasal verb is a verb plus a particle, usually a preposition or adverb, that together make a new meaning. Look up does not mean to look in an upward direction; it means to search for information. Give up does not mean to give something upward; it means to stop trying. The particle changes the verb completely, so you must learn the combination as one unit, not as two separate words.

Some phrasal verbs are separable: you can put the object between the verb and the particle. Turn off the light and Turn the light off are both correct. Others are inseparable: the object must come after the whole phrase. Look after the children is correct, but Look the children after is wrong. When you learn a new phrasal verb, always note whether it is separable and whether it takes an object. That small note prevents many errors.

Twenty Phrasal Verbs for Daily Use

Here are twenty expressions worth mastering first: wake up, get up, put on, take off, look for, look after, turn on, turn off, pick up, drop off, set up, clean up, give up, carry on, find out, work out, run out of, catch up, slow down, and hang out. Each one appears in daily conversation. I need to find out the homework. We ran out of sugar. Please slow down and explain again. Learning these twenty gives you coverage for a large share of everyday talk.

Do not try to memorise all twenty in one sitting. Learn five at a time, use each one in two sentences of your own, and review them the next day. Group them by situation: morning routine, school, home, and free time. For example, morning routine gives wake up, get up, put on, and set off. School gives catch up, work out, find out, and hand in. Grouping by situation makes recall easier because the words are linked to a real scene.

  • Morning: wake up, get up, put on, set off.
  • School: catch up, work out, find out, hand in.
  • Home: clean up, turn off, look after, pick up.
  • Plans: give up, carry on, run out of, slow down.

Short Dialogues You Can Reuse

Dialogue one, at home: Did you turn off the fan? Yes, I turned it off before leaving. Dialogue two, at school: I missed yesterday. Do not worry, we will help you catch up. Dialogue three, with a friend: I cannot work out this sum. Let us look at it together and find out where it went wrong. Notice how each dialogue uses the phrasal verb in a natural, short exchange. That is how you should practise: in two-line dialogues, not in isolated lists.

Try writing your own dialogues for five phrasal verbs each week. Keep them short and realistic. Act them out with a partner if you can. Speaking an expression aloud fixes it in memory far better than silent reading. For more speaking and writing practice, see writing natural dialogue and story writing.

Idioms: Use a Few, Use Them Well

An idiom is a fixed expression whose meaning cannot be guessed from its words. A piece of cake means something easy, not a slice of cake. Break the ice means to ease awkwardness at the start of a meeting, not to smash ice. Idioms add colour, but they are risky. Used wrongly, they confuse the reader. Used too often, they make writing sound artificial.

The safe rule is to learn idioms in context and use only the ones you are sure about. Start with five reliable idioms: a piece of cake, under the weather, once in a blue moon, cost an arm and a leg, and on cloud nine. Use each one in a full sentence and check that the situation fits. In examinations, one well-placed idiom is better than five forced ones. Clarity always beats decoration.

How to Practise Without Mixing Meanings

Keep a small notebook with three columns: phrasal verb or idiom, meaning in simple English, and a sentence of your own. Review it for five minutes a day. When you meet a new expression in reading, add it to the notebook instead of trying to memorise it on the spot. Test yourself by covering the meaning and recalling it from the sentence.

Avoid translating literally from your first language. Many phrasal verbs have no direct equivalent, and literal translation produces errors. Learn the English meaning through an English example. If you are unsure, check a learner dictionary that gives example sentences. For more English help, browse the books page and the solved books page.

5 key points you must know

  • A phrasal verb is a verb plus particle with a new combined meaning.
  • Learn whether each phrasal verb is separable or inseparable.
  • Master twenty daily phrasal verbs before learning rare ones.
  • Practise in short two-line dialogues, not isolated lists.
  • Use idioms sparingly and only when you are sure of the meaning.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between look for and look after?

Look for means to search for something: I am looking for my book. Look after means to take care of someone: She looks after her younger brother.

Can I put the object between the verb and particle?

Only for separable phrasal verbs. Turn off the light and Turn the light off are both correct. For inseparable verbs like look after, the object must come after the whole phrase.

How many idioms should I use in an essay?

One or two well-placed idioms are enough. Too many idioms make writing sound forced. Clarity and correct grammar matter more than decoration.

Why does literal translation fail for idioms?

Idioms have a fixed figurative meaning that is not the sum of their words. A piece of cake means easy, not a slice of cake. Learn idioms through English examples, not word-by-word translation.
