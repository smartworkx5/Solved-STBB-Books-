Punjab Second Annual First-Week Plan: English, Maths, Biology and More

By Solved Sindh Textbook Board Books Team · Updated 2026-11-03 · 1604 words · 8 min read

The first week of the Punjab second annual examinations is where good planning pays off most. With Part-II theory papers running from 3 to 16 November 2026, the opening days set your rhythm for the whole series, and the gaps between papers decide how much real revision you can fit in. This plan uses subject examples from the BISE Multan date sheet to show how a first week can look, but it comes with one warning that matters more than any timetable: every student must confirm their own board's date sheet, because subject dates can differ from board to board. Use the structure here, and fill in your own board's exact dates.

How to Read Your Date Sheet Before Planning Anything

A date sheet is more than a list of dates. It tells you the order of your papers, the gaps between them, and whether each paper falls in the morning session at 9:00 AM or the evening session at 2:00 PM, with Friday evening papers at 2:30 PM. Start by writing out your own papers in order, with the gap in days before each one, and you will immediately see which papers have breathing room and which ones arrive back to back. That gap map is the real foundation of your plan, because a paper with a three-day gap can carry a full revision cycle, while a paper that follows the previous one by a single day needs short, targeted revision only.

The examples in this article come from the BISE Multan date sheet, and they are useful for showing the shape of a first week, but they are not a substitute for your own board's sheet. If your board places Chemistry before Computer Science, or your Physics paper falls on a different day, your plan must follow your board, not Multan's. Check your roll number slip and your board's published date sheet, and if the two ever disagree, ask your school or college office which one to trust. Planning from the wrong sheet is one of the few mistakes that can cost a whole paper.

English First: The 4 November Paper

In the BISE Multan examples, 12th class English Compulsory falls on 4 November, very early in the series. English is a paper where many students lose marks not on knowledge but on presentation and time management, so the revision for it should look different from a science paper. Practise one essay and one precis-style passage under timed conditions, revise the grammar rules that repeatedly appear, and read through your textbook lessons once more for the short questions. Keep a list of quotations, pair-of-words and correction items that you keep getting wrong, and drill only that list in the final day.

On the day itself, attempt the sections you are strongest in first, and leave enough time for the essay, which carries heavy marks and cannot be rushed in the last ten minutes. Write neatly, keep paragraphs short, and check spellings of common words as you go. An English paper rewards steady, organised writing far more than it rewards cramming new material at the last hour, so treat the day before it as a practice day, not a reading day. Mathematics and Biology on 6 November

The Multan examples place Mathematics and Biology on 6 November, which means students of either stream face a heavy paper two days after English. For Mathematics, the final revision should be almost entirely practice: work through past-paper style questions from each chapter, write every step clearly, and keep a short formula sheet that you revise from memory rather than reading passively. The most common Mathematics mistakes are skipped steps, sign errors and unfinished questions, and all three are fixed by practice under time pressure, not by re-reading solved examples.

For Biology, the same two days should be spent on diagrams, definitions and the short questions that examiners repeat year after year. Draw each important diagram from memory at least once, label it fully, and practise writing definitions in your own words rather than memorising long paragraphs word for word. Biology answers score best when they are precise and well labelled, so spend your last revision hour on the diagrams and terminology that you find hardest to recall. Both streams should sleep properly the night before, because a tired mind makes exactly the small errors that cost marks in these papers.

Computer Science, Chemistry and Physics Across the Week

Computer Science appears on 10 November in the Multan examples, giving students a four-day gap after the 6 November papers, which is generous by exam standards. Use the first two days of that gap for the chapters you find hardest, especially programming concepts and short questions, and the last two days for full practice papers and revision of definitions. Chemistry on 12 November follows a similar pattern: split the gap between physical, organic and inorganic portions, and practise numerical problems and chemical equations by writing them out, since Chemistry rewards students who can reproduce equations accurately under pressure.

Physics on 16 November closes the Part-II theory series in these examples, and it is the paper students most often underprepare because it comes last. Do not let the long gap breed laziness. Work through numerical problems chapter by chapter, revise derivations by writing them from memory, and practise the short questions that test understanding rather than memory. A useful rule for the whole week is to finish each day's revision by writing down tomorrow's plan, so that every morning starts with a clear task instead of a decision about what to study.

Rest, Health and the Warning That Protects Your Plan

A first-week plan fails more often from exhaustion than from a lack of study. Eat regular meals, keep water nearby during revision, and protect at least six to seven hours of sleep each night, because the brain consolidates what it studied only during rest. Short breaks every hour keep concentration higher than marathon sessions do, and a walk or light exercise between papers helps more than an extra hour of tired reading. If you feel overwhelmed, scale the plan down rather than abandoning it: a smaller plan followed properly beats a perfect plan followed for one day.

Finally, repeat the warning that underpins this whole article. Every date in it, from English on 4 November to Physics on 16 November, is a BISE Multan example, given to show the shape of a first week. Your own board, whether Lahore, Rawalpindi, Gujranwala, Faisalabad, Sargodha, Sahiwal, Bahawalpur or DG Khan, may place your papers on different days. Confirm your own board's date sheet before you lock your timetable, and if anything about your centre or timing is unclear, check with your school or college office rather than relying on a friend's sheet. A plan built on your own verified dates is the only plan worth following.

5 key points you must know

  • Part-II theory papers run 3 to 16 November 2026; always confirm your own board's date sheet
  • BISE Multan examples: English 4 Nov, Mathematics or Biology 6 Nov, Computer Science 10 Nov
  • Chemistry follows on 12 Nov and Physics on 16 Nov in the Multan examples
  • Use gaps between papers for practice and weak chapters, not passive re-reading
  • Protect sleep and short breaks; exhaustion costs more marks than a missed topic

Frequently asked questions

Are the dates in this plan the same for every Punjab board?

No. The dates given here are BISE Multan date-sheet examples used to show the shape of a first week. Subject dates can differ between the nine Punjab boards, so every student must confirm their own board's published date sheet and roll number slip.

How should I revise English the day before the paper?

Practise one essay and one passage under timed conditions, drill the grammar items you keep getting wrong, and read through textbook lessons for short questions. English rewards organised, neat writing, so treat the last day as practice, not new reading.

What is the best way to use a four-day gap before Computer Science or Chemistry?

Spend the first half on your weakest chapters and the second half on full practice papers and definitions. For Chemistry, practise equations and numerical problems by writing them out, since accuracy under pressure is what scores.

Why does Physics need attention even though it comes last?

Because a long gap can breed laziness, and Physics needs steady numerical practice. Work through problems chapter by chapter, revise derivations from memory, and keep the short conceptual questions in daily revision.

What if my board's date sheet differs from the examples here?

Follow your own board's sheet. If your roll number slip and a published sheet ever disagree, ask your school or college office which one to trust rather than guessing or copying a friend's timetable.
