Maths Word Problems: Profit and Loss in a Shop

By Solved Sindh Textbook Board Books Team · Updated 2026-11-04 · 1847 words · 9 min read

Every shopkeeper buys at one price and sells at another, and the difference between those two prices is either a profit or a loss. Profit and loss problems test whether you can identify the cost price and the selling price, find the difference, and express it as a percentage of the cost price. Students lose marks mainly by calculating the percentage on the selling price instead of the cost price, or by mixing up which price is which. This guide explains cost price, selling price and profit percent with shop examples, and shows you the checks that keep your answers correct. More practice is in our [solved notes](/solved.html) and on the [books page](/books.html).

Cost Price and Selling Price

The cost price is what the shopkeeper pays to buy an item, and the selling price is what the customer pays to buy it from the shop. If the selling price is higher than the cost price, the shopkeeper makes a profit; if it is lower, the shopkeeper makes a loss; if they are equal, there is neither profit nor loss. For example, a shopkeeper buys a toy for 400 rupees and sells it for 500 rupees, making a profit of 100 rupees, because 500 minus 400 equals 100. If instead the toy is sold for 350 rupees, the loss is 50 rupees, because 400 minus 350 equals 50. Profit and loss are always found by subtracting the smaller price from the larger, but you must label which one it is.

A common confusion is treating the marked price as the cost price, but these are different. The marked price is the price written on the tag, while the cost price is what the shopkeeper actually paid the supplier. A shopkeeper might buy a shirt for 600 rupees, mark it at 900 rupees, and sell it at a discount for 800 rupees, making a profit of 200 rupees on a cost price of 600. The marked price matters only for discount problems; for profit and loss, only the cost price and the selling price count. Always underline both prices in the question before calculating anything.

Finding Profit or Loss

To find profit or loss, subtract the cost price from the selling price: a positive result is a profit, and a negative result means a loss of that amount. A shopkeeper buys 10 pens for 200 rupees and sells them for 300 rupees, making a profit of 100 rupees in total, or 10 rupees per pen. If the same pens are sold for 150 rupees, the loss is 50 rupees in total, or 5 rupees per pen. When quantities are involved, find the total cost price and the total selling price first, then subtract; working per item also works, but mixing total and per-item figures in one calculation is a frequent source of errors.

Some problems give the profit or loss and one price, and ask for the other price. If a book bought for 250 rupees is sold at a profit of 50 rupees, the selling price is 250 plus 50, which is 300 rupees. If a bag bought for 800 rupees is sold at a loss of 100 rupees, the selling price is 800 minus 100, which is 700 rupees. The rule is simple: add the profit to the cost price, or subtract the loss from the cost price, to get the selling price. Drawing a small diagram with cost price, selling price and the gap between them helps you see whether to add or subtract.

Profit Percent Problems

Profit percent is the profit expressed as a percentage of the cost price, never of the selling price. The formula is profit divided by cost price, multiplied by 100. If a toy bought for 400 rupees is sold for 500 rupees, the profit is 100 rupees, and the profit percent is 100 divided by 400 times 100, which is 25 percent. This is the single most tested rule in the topic, and examiners deliberately set questions where using the selling price gives a tempting but wrong answer: 100 divided by 500 times 100 would give 20 percent, which is incorrect. Always divide by the cost price.

Profit percent problems also work backwards: given the cost price and the profit percent, find the selling price. If a shirt costs 600 rupees and is sold at a profit of 20 percent, the profit is 20 percent of 600, which is 120 rupees, so the selling price is 720 rupees. The shortcut is to multiply the cost price by 120 percent, which is 1.20 times 600, also giving 720 rupees. The two-step method is safer for beginners because it shows the profit amount separately, which the question often asks for as well as the selling price.

Loss Percent and Selling at a Loss

Loss percent follows the same rule as profit percent: loss divided by cost price, multiplied by 100. If a phone bought for 10000 rupees is sold for 9000 rupees, the loss is 1000 rupees, and the loss percent is 1000 divided by 10000 times 100, which is 10 percent. If the loss percent and the cost price are given, find the loss amount first and subtract it: a bicycle costing 5000 rupees sold at a loss of 8 percent loses 400 rupees, so the selling price is 4600 rupees. The shortcut is to multiply by 92 percent, which is 0.92 times 5000, also giving 4600 rupees.

A classic exam trap asks for the cost price when the selling price and the profit or loss percent are given. If a watch is sold for 1100 rupees at a profit of 10 percent, the cost price is not 1100 minus 10 percent of 1100. Instead, the selling price represents 110 percent of the cost price, so the cost price is 1100 divided by 1.10, which is 1000 rupees. The same logic applies to losses: a selling price of 900 rupees at a loss of 10 percent means the selling price is 90 percent of the cost price, so the cost price is 900 divided by 0.90, which is 1000 rupees. Learning this reverse method earns marks on the hardest questions.

Common Mistakes in Profit and Loss Problems

The first common mistake is calculating profit or loss percent on the selling price instead of the cost price, which is wrong in every case. The second is confusing cost price with marked price, especially when a discount is also mentioned in the question. The third is mixing total and per-item figures, such as comparing the total cost of 10 pens with the selling price of one pen. The fourth is subtracting in the wrong order and calling a profit a loss, or giving a negative profit percent. The fifth is finding the profit amount correctly but stopping there when the question asked for the profit percent.

To avoid these errors, always start by writing cost price equals and selling price equals with their values, then state profit or loss, and only then calculate the percent. Check that a profit percent is positive when the selling price exceeds the cost price, and that a loss percent appears when it does not. Finally, reread the question to confirm whether it asked for the amount, the percent, or the missing price. With this method, profit and loss problems become steady, predictable marks. 5 key points you must know

  • Cost price is what the shopkeeper pays; selling price is what the customer pays.
  • Profit equals selling price minus cost price; a lower selling price means a loss.
  • Profit percent and loss percent are always calculated on the cost price, never the selling price.
  • To find a selling price from a profit percent, add the profit to the cost price.
  • To find a cost price from a selling price, divide by 1 plus or minus the percent as a decimal.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between cost price and selling price?

The cost price is the amount the shopkeeper pays to buy an item from a supplier, while the selling price is the amount the customer pays the shopkeeper. If the selling price is higher, the shopkeeper makes a profit; if it is lower, the shopkeeper makes a loss. The marked price on the tag is a third, different value that matters for discounts but not for profit and loss calculations, which use only cost price and selling price.

How do I calculate profit percent?

First find the profit by subtracting the cost price from the selling price, then divide the profit by the cost price and multiply by 100. For example, buying at 400 rupees and selling at 500 gives a profit of 100 rupees and a profit percent of 25 percent. Dividing by the selling price instead would give 20 percent, which is the most common error in this topic, so always divide by the cost price.

How do I find the selling price from a profit percent?

Find the profit amount as the given percent of the cost price, then add it to the cost price. If a shirt costs 600 rupees and is sold at a profit of 20 percent, the profit is 120 rupees and the selling price is 720 rupees. The shortcut is to multiply the cost price by 1.20, but the two-step method is safer because it shows the profit amount separately, which the question often asks for as well.

How do I find the cost price from a selling price and profit percent?

Remember that the selling price represents 100 percent plus the profit percent of the cost price. If a watch sells for 1100 rupees at a profit of 10 percent, the selling price is 110 percent of the cost price, so the cost price is 1100 divided by 1.10, which is 1000 rupees. For a loss, the selling price is 100 percent minus the loss percent, so divide by 0.90 for a 10 percent loss.

Why do profit and loss answers go wrong?

Most errors come from calculating the percent on the selling price instead of the cost price, confusing cost price with marked price, mixing total and per-item figures, or stopping at the profit amount when the question asked for the percent. Writing cost price and selling price explicitly, stating profit or loss before calculating the percent, and rereading the question at the end will prevent nearly all of these mistakes.
