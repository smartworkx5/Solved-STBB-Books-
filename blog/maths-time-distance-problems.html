Maths Word Problems: Time and Distance

By Tasleem Sanjrani · SolvedBooks.pk · 1679 words · 8 min read

Time and distance problems appear in almost every maths exam because they test whether you can connect three simple ideas: how far something travels, how fast it moves, and how long the journey takes. Students often know the formulas but still lose marks because they mix up units, forget to convert minutes into hours, or answer a different question from the one asked. The good news is that every time and distance problem uses the same three-way relationship, so once you learn to organise the given information, the problems become routine. This guide explains speed, journey time and late-or-early problems with worked examples, and shows you the checks that catch careless errors. The Three Friends: Speed, Distance and Time

Speed, distance and time are linked by one simple rule: distance equals speed multiplied by time. From this one rule you can find any of the three if you know the other two. Speed equals distance divided by time, and time equals distance divided by speed. A useful way to remember this is the triangle method: cover the quantity you want to find, and the remaining two show the operation. Cover distance and you see speed times time; cover speed and you see distance over time; cover time and you see distance over speed. Write this triangle at the top of your rough work before starting any problem.

The most important rule in these problems is that units must match. If speed is given in kilometres per hour, then time must be in hours and distance in kilometres. If time is given in minutes, convert it to hours by dividing by 60 before using it with a speed in kilometres per hour. For example, 30 minutes is 0.5 hours, and 45 minutes is 0.75 hours. Many students skip this conversion and get answers that are 60 times too big or too small. Always write the units beside every number, and convert first, calculate second.

Journey Time Problems

A journey time problem usually gives you the distance and the speed and asks how long the trip takes. Divide the distance by the speed, and remember to convert the answer into hours and minutes if it comes out as a decimal. For example, a bus travels 120 kilometres at a speed of 40 kilometres per hour. The time is 120 divided by 40, which is 3 hours. If a car travels 150 kilometres at 50 kilometres per hour, the time is 150 divided by 50, which is also 3 hours. When the answer is a decimal like 2.5 hours, convert it properly: 2.5 hours means 2 hours and 30 minutes, not 2 hours and 50 minutes.

Some journey problems give the starting time and ask for the arrival time, which means you must add the journey time to the start time. If a train leaves at 8:15 in the morning and the journey takes 2 hours and 45 minutes, it arrives at 11:00. Add the hours first, then the minutes, and carry over 60 minutes as one hour whenever the minutes reach 60 or more. Practise adding times on a clock face in your head, because clock arithmetic is where most students slip. If the journey crosses noon, be careful with morning and afternoon labels, and write am or pm beside your final answer.

Late or Early: Comparing Two Journeys

Late-or-early problems compare two journeys over the same distance at different speeds, and ask who arrives first or how many minutes late someone is. The method is to calculate each journey time separately and then subtract the smaller from the larger. For example, Ali walks 4 kilometres to school at 4 kilometres per hour, taking 1 hour, while his friend cycles the same distance at 8 kilometres per hour, taking half an hour. The cyclist arrives 30 minutes earlier. Always calculate both times fully before comparing; guessing from the speeds alone often leads to errors when distances differ.

A trickier version gives a fixed arrival time and asks what speed is needed to arrive on time. Suppose school is 6 kilometres away and a student must arrive in 30 minutes, which is 0.5 hours. The required speed is 6 divided by 0.5, which is 12 kilometres per hour. If the student walks at only 6 kilometres per hour, the journey takes 1 hour, so the student would be 30 minutes late. These problems reward students who write both journeys in a small table with columns for person, speed and time, because the table makes the comparison visible and prevents mixing up the two journeys.

Unit Changes: Minutes, Hours and Kilometres

Unit conversion is the hidden skill inside time and distance problems, and it causes more lost marks than the formulas themselves. Remember the key conversions: 1 hour equals 60 minutes, 1 minute equals 60 seconds, and 1 kilometre equals 1000 metres. To change minutes into hours, divide by 60; to change hours into minutes, multiply by 60. For example, 90 minutes is 1.5 hours, and 2.25 hours is 2 hours and 15 minutes. When a speed is given in metres per second but the distance is in kilometres, convert one of them first so both match.

A common exam question asks for speed in kilometres per hour when distance is in metres and time in seconds, which needs two conversions at once. The safest route is to convert distance to kilometres and time to hours first, then divide. Alternatively, remember that multiplying a speed in metres per second by 3.6 gives kilometres per hour, and dividing kilometres per hour by 3.6 gives metres per second. Learn this 3.6 rule only after you understand the two-step conversion, because examiners often ask you to show the conversion working. Always write the unit beside your final answer, such as 40 kilometres per hour.

Common Mistakes in Time and Distance Problems

The biggest mistake is using minutes directly with a speed in kilometres per hour without converting to hours, which gives an answer 60 times too large. The second is writing 2.5 hours as 2 hours and 50 minutes instead of 2 hours and 30 minutes, because the decimal part is a fraction of an hour, not minutes. The third is forgetting to add the journey time to the starting time when the question asks for arrival time, and instead giving only the journey time as the answer. The fourth is mixing up which quantity to divide by which, such as dividing speed by distance instead of distance by speed.

To avoid these errors, always start by listing what is given with units, then write the formula you will use, then convert units, and only then calculate. After finishing, do a sense check: a person walking cannot travel at 60 kilometres per hour, and a bus travelling 120 kilometres at 40 kilometres per hour cannot take 30 hours. If your answer looks impossible, recheck your conversions first, because that is where the error usually hides. With steady practice, time and distance problems become quick, reliable marks. 5 key points you must know

  • Distance equals speed times time; cover one value in the triangle to find the formula.
  • Units must match: convert minutes to hours before using kilometres per hour.
  • A decimal like 2.5 hours means 2 hours and 30 minutes, not 2 hours and 50 minutes.
  • For late-or-early problems, calculate both journey times fully, then subtract.
  • Always sense-check your answer: impossible speeds or times signal a conversion error.

Frequently asked questions

What is the formula for speed, distance and time?

Distance equals speed multiplied by time, and from this one rule you can find any value if you know the other two. Speed equals distance divided by time, and time equals distance divided by speed. A handy memory aid is the triangle: cover the value you want, and the remaining two show the operation. Always make sure the units match before calculating, converting minutes to hours when speed is in kilometres per hour.

How do I convert minutes into hours in a problem?

Divide the number of minutes by 60 to get hours. For example, 30 minutes is 0.5 hours, 45 minutes is 0.75 hours, and 90 minutes is 1.5 hours. This step is essential whenever speed is given in kilometres per hour, because using minutes directly gives an answer 60 times too large. To change hours back into minutes, multiply by 60 instead.

How do I find arrival time from a start time and journey time?

Add the journey time to the starting time, adding hours first and then minutes, and carrying over 60 minutes as one extra hour whenever the minutes reach 60 or more. For example, leaving at 8:15 in the morning for a journey of 2 hours and 45 minutes gives an arrival time of 11:00. Be careful with morning and afternoon labels when a journey crosses noon, and write am or pm beside your final answer.

What is a late-or-early problem in maths?

A late-or-early problem compares two journeys over the same or different distances and asks who arrives first or by how many minutes someone is late. Calculate each journey time separately using time equals distance divided by speed, then subtract the smaller time from the larger to find the difference. Writing both journeys in a small table with columns for person, speed and time helps you avoid mixing up the two journeys.

Why are my time and distance answers often wrong?

Most errors come from unit mistakes, such as using minutes with kilometres per hour, or misreading a decimal like 2.5 hours as 2 hours and 50 minutes. Other common errors are dividing the wrong way round and forgetting to add journey time to the start time when arrival time is asked. Listing given values with units, writing the formula first, converting units, and sense-checking the final answer will fix nearly all of these mistakes.
