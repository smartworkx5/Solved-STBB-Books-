English Idioms for Students: 20 With Meanings

By Tasleem Sanjrani · SolvedBooks.pk · 1534 words · 8 min read

Idioms confuse students when learned as word lists, but they stick when learned as stories: break the ice, a piece of cake, hit the books and seventeen more idioms each come here with a simple meaning and a small picture story that explains why the phrase means what it does. Learn four idioms at a time, retell each story in your own words, and write one sentence with each idiom, because a story plus a sentence fixes an idiom far better than repetition alone. Practise through the [solved notes](/solved.html) and [books](/books.html) pages, and use idioms sparingly in essays, where one well-placed idiom impresses more than five forced ones.

Idioms That Break the Ice

Idioms are phrases with a special meaning that differs from their separate words, and learning them with a small story makes them stick, which is why this guide pairs every idiom with a story scene, starting with friendly idioms on blog. Break the ice means to start friendly talk in a shy group. Picture a new student who tells a joke on the first day, and suddenly everyone talks and laughs, because the ice of shyness has broken. A piece of cake means something very easy to do. Picture a student who finishes a simple sum in one minute and smiles, because the task felt as easy as eating a piece of cake.

More friendly idioms describe learning and weather in a colourful way, and once you picture the scene, the meaning stays even when the words themselves sound strange at first hearing. Hit the books means to study hard. Picture a student who opens books the night before a test and studies with full attention, because hitting the books means serious study, not hitting anything real. Under the weather means feeling slightly ill. Picture a student who feels tired and dull on a cloudy morning and stays home, because being under the weather means feeling unwell.

Idioms About Time and Luck

Time and luck idioms appear often in stories and essays, because once in a blue moon, raining cats and dogs and similar phrases paint time, weather and fortune in a memorable picture that plain words cannot match. Once in a blue moon means very rarely. Picture a blue moon that appears almost never in the sky, just like a visit from a faraway cousin that happens once in a blue moon. Raining cats and dogs means raining very heavily. Picture rain falling so hard that people joke about cats and dogs falling too, because the rain feels wild and unstoppable.

Luck idioms also teach courage and patience, because bite the bullet and beat around the bush describe facing difficulty directly or avoiding it, and students meet both behaviours in stories every term. Bite the bullet means to face something unpleasant bravely. Picture a brave student who admits a mistake honestly instead of hiding it, because biting the bullet means facing trouble with courage. Beat around the bush means to avoid saying something directly. Picture a student who talks about everything except the lost book, because beating around the bush means avoiding the real point.

Idioms About Cost and Secrets

Cost and secret idioms help you describe money and talk in a lively way, because cost an arm and a leg and let the cat out of the bag turn ordinary ideas into pictures that readers remember long after reading. Cost an arm and a leg means to be very expensive. Picture a bag so costly that buying it feels like giving an arm and a leg, because the price is far too high for a student. Let the cat out of the bag means to reveal a secret by mistake. Picture a birthday surprise ruined when a brother mentions the gift early, because he let the cat out of the bag.

More idioms describe choices and chances, because the ball is in your court tells a friend that the next move is theirs, and burn the midnight oil describes the student who studies late into the night before a big day. The ball is in your court means it is your turn to act. Picture a tennis player waiting while the ball rests on your side, because the next move belongs to you and no one else. Burn the midnight oil means to study or work late at night. Picture a lamp burning past midnight while a student revises lessons, because the oil in the lamp burns during late-night work.

Idioms About Life and Trouble

Life idioms describe good fortune and hard times in one picture, because a blessing in disguise and through thick and thin speak about hidden good and lasting friendship better than long explanations ever could. A blessing in disguise means a good thing hidden in bad luck. Picture a cancelled trip that leads to a happy family day at home, because the bad luck hid a blessing in disguise. Through thick and thin means through good and bad times together. Picture two friends who stay together in success and failure, because true friendship survives through thick and thin.

Trouble idioms warn and comfort at once, because add insult to injury and by hook or by crook describe making trouble worse or succeeding by any fair effort, and students should use them carefully in writing. Add insult to injury means to make a bad situation worse. Picture a student who loses a book and then gets blamed unfairly too, because the blame adds insult to injury. By hook or by crook means by any possible means. Picture a determined student who finishes homework by hook or by crook, using every honest method available before bedtime.

Idioms for Exams and Daily Talk

Exam and daily-talk idioms finish this set with pictures students can use at once, from in hot water for trouble to jump on the bandwagon for following a trend, with more idiom practice on solved notes for confident writers. In hot water means in trouble. Picture a student who forgot homework and faces the teacher, because being in hot water means being in trouble. Jump on the bandwagon means to join something popular. Picture students joining a reading club only because everyone else joined, because jumping on the bandwagon means following a trend.

The last pair rewards wise writing, because kill two birds with one stone and the best of both worlds describe smart planning and happy balance, and using them well shows maturity in essays and speeches. Kill two birds with one stone means to achieve two things with one action. Picture a student who revises vocabulary while walking to school, because one walk achieves exercise and learning, killing two birds with one stone. The best of both worlds means the advantages of two different situations. Picture a student who enjoys village peace and city libraries together, because that is the best of both worlds.

5 key points you must know

  • Learn idioms with stories, not as bare word lists.
  • Four idioms at a time is a strong, steady pace.
  • Retell each idiom story in your own words.
  • Write one sentence with every idiom you learn.
  • Use idioms sparingly in essays: one well placed is enough.

Frequently asked questions

What is an idiom?

An idiom is a phrase whose meaning differs from its separate words, such as break the ice, which means to start friendly talk, not to break real ice, so idioms must be learned as whole phrases with their special meanings. Keep this answer beside your lesson notes, and read it once more before the next test so the idea stays fresh and ready.

How many idioms should I learn before a test?

Twenty well-learned idioms with stories and sentences help more than fifty half-remembered ones, because tests reward correct use in sentences, and a confident idiom used correctly earns more than a guessed one used wrongly. Use the idea in one real sentence or task today, because using knowledge turns it from a fact into a skill you own.

Can I use idioms in formal letters?

Avoid idioms in formal letters and applications, because formal writing needs clear, respectful language, while idioms suit essays, stories, speeches and friendly writing where colour and personality are welcome. Revise older answers together with new ones, so new learning connects with what you already know instead of replacing it.

What if I forget the meaning of an idiom?

Recall its story picture first, because the story usually carries the meaning, and if the story fails too, check your notebook and write a fresh sentence, since one new sentence often restores the meaning at once. Stay patient with slow progress, because steady small steps in English build more lasting skill than rushed cramming before tests.

How do I avoid using idioms wrongly?

Use an idiom only when you know its exact meaning and situation, copy the example sentence pattern at first, and ask a teacher to check early attempts, because one correct use builds confidence while one wrong use creates confusion. End each study session by recalling the main point aloud without looking, since recall practice strengthens memory far more than re-reading.
