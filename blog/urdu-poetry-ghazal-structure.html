Urdu Poetry: Ghazal Structure Explained

By Solved Sindh Textbook Board Books Team · Updated 2026-11-04 · 1605 words · 8 min read

The ghazal is the most famous form of Urdu poetry, and its beauty comes from a strict but musical structure that poets have followed for centuries. Many students enjoy ghazals without knowing the names of their parts, but learning four simple terms, matla, maqta, radif and qafia, turns listening into real understanding and makes exam questions much easier. This article explains each part of the ghazal in plain language, gives famous examples, and shows how students can analyse any ghazal step by step.

What Is a Ghazal?

A ghazal is a poem made of couplets called ash'aar, where each couplet is usually complete in itself and can express a different thought, feeling or image. A ghazal normally has at least five couplets, and it follows strict rules of rhyme and rhythm, yet it still feels free because each couplet can move to a new idea. The ghazal traditionally speaks of love, separation, longing and the mysteries of life, but modern poets also use it for social and philosophical themes. Its music comes from the repeated rhyme pattern, which makes ghazals easy to sing and remember.

For students, the ghazal is important because it appears in Urdu lessons, in songs, and in general knowledge, and because its structure is asked about in exams. Unlike a nazam, a ghazal does not need one central idea across all couplets, so a student can enjoy and explain one couplet at a time without understanding the whole poem. This independence of couplets is also why famous ghazal lines are quoted alone in daily conversation. Our solved notes and blog offer simple guides to other poetry forms as well.

Matla: The Opening Couplet

The matla is the first couplet of a ghazal, and it is special because both of its lines end with the same rhyme pattern, which sets the music for the whole poem. Every later couplet follows the pattern fixed by the matla, so the opening couplet works like the first step that decides the direction of the dance. A famous example is Ghalib's opening: Dil-e-nadan tujhe hua kya hai, aakhir is dard ki dawa kya hai, where both lines end with the same sound pattern, and that pattern then repeats through the ghazal. Students can spot the matla easily because it is always the first couplet and both its lines rhyme.

The matla also introduces the mood of the ghazal, whether it will be sad, thoughtful, romantic or philosophical, and a good matla makes the listener want to hear more. Poets take special care with the matla because a weak opening can spoil the whole ghazal, while a strong one creates an instant connection. In exams, a question may ask students to identify the matla, and the answer is simply the first couplet with a note that both lines carry the rhyme. Practising with famous ghazals helps students recognise the matla at a glance, and our books page lists poetry material by class.

Radif and Qafia: The Music of the Ghazal

Radif is the word or phrase that repeats exactly at the end of every couplet in a ghazal, like a chorus that returns again and again. Qafia is the rhyming word that comes just before the radif and changes in each couplet while keeping the same sound, like different words that rhyme with each other. Together, radif and qafia create the ghazal's music: the radif gives unity because it never changes, and the qafia gives variety because it brings a new rhyming word each time. A simple way to remember is that radif repeats exactly, while qafia only rhymes.

Consider a ghazal where every second line ends with the same repeated phrase; that phrase is the radif, and the words just before it that rhyme with each other are the qafia. Poets choose their radif carefully because a long radif is harder to maintain, while a short radif gives more freedom, and a clever qafia shows the poet's skill with words. Students do not need to master these terms perfectly at first; they only need to find the repeated ending and the rhyming words before it. This skill is asked about in exams, and it becomes easy after practising with two or three famous ghazals from our solved notes.

Maqta: The Poet's Signature

The maqta is the last couplet of a ghazal, and it is special because the poet mentions his own pen name, or takhallus, inside it. This is like a signature at the end of a painting, and it tells the listener who created the ghazal. A famous example is Ghalib's closing couplet: Humko maloom hai jannat ki haqeeqat lekin, dil ke khush rakhne ko Ghalib khayal achha hai, where the word Ghalib appears in the second line as the poet's signature. Students can spot the maqta because it is the last couplet and it carries the poet's pen name.

The maqta is often clever or playful, because the poet speaks about himself, his fame, his pain or his art, and sometimes he jokes about his own condition. It gives the ghazal a personal ending after a series of general couplets, and listeners often wait for it with special interest. In exams, students may be asked to identify the maqta or the takhallus, and the answer is the last couplet with the pen name named clearly. Remembering one famous maqta, like Ghalib's, is enough to understand the concept for life.

How to Analyse a Ghazal in an Exam

Analysing a ghazal in an exam follows a simple order: name the matla, identify the radif and qafia, explain one or two couplets in plain words, and name the maqta with the poet's takhallus. This order shows the examiner that the student understands both the structure and the meaning, and it keeps the answer organised instead of rambling. Students should quote couplets correctly and briefly, because a wrong quotation loses marks even when the explanation is good. Writing the structure first and the meanings second is a smart strategy that works for most ghazal questions.

Students should also practise by taking a famous ghazal, marking its matla, radif, qafia and maqta with coloured pens, and writing one-line meanings for each couplet. This single exercise teaches structure, vocabulary and meaning at the same time, and it can be repeated with a new ghazal every week. 5 key points you must know

  • A ghazal is a poem of couplets, each usually complete in itself, with at least five couplets.
  • The matla is the first couplet, where both lines carry the same rhyme pattern.
  • Radif repeats exactly at each couplet's end, while qafia only rhymes before it.
  • The maqta is the last couplet and carries the poet's pen name, or takhallus.
  • Analyse a ghazal in order: matla, radif and qafia, couplet meanings, then maqta.

Frequently asked questions

What is the matla of a ghazal?

The matla is the first couplet of a ghazal, and both of its lines end with the same rhyme pattern, which fixes the music for the whole poem. Every later couplet follows the pattern set by the matla, so it works like the opening step of a dance. Students can identify it easily because it is always the first couplet and both lines rhyme.

What is the difference between radif and qafia?

Radif is the word or phrase that repeats exactly at the end of every couplet in a ghazal, while qafia is the rhyming word that comes just before the radif and changes in each couplet. The radif gives unity because it never changes, and the qafia gives variety because it brings a new rhyming word each time. Remember: radif repeats exactly, qafia only rhymes.

What is the maqta and how do I find it?

The maqta is the last couplet of a ghazal, and the poet mentions his pen name, or takhallus, inside it, like a signature. Ghalib's famous maqta includes his own name in the second line, which makes it easy to spot. To find a maqta, look at the last couplet and check whether the poet's pen name appears in it.

How many couplets does a ghazal usually have?

A ghazal usually has at least five couplets, and many famous ghazals have between seven and twelve couplets. There is no strict upper limit, but poets usually keep ghazals long enough to explore a mood and short enough to stay musical. Each couplet is normally complete in itself, so a ghazal can be enjoyed one couplet at a time.

How can I learn ghazal structure quickly?

Take one famous ghazal and mark its parts with coloured pens: underline the matla, circle the radif, mark the qafia words, and highlight the maqta with the poet's pen name. Then write a one-line meaning for each couplet. Repeating this exercise with a new ghazal every week teaches structure, vocabulary and meaning together, and builds lasting confidence.
