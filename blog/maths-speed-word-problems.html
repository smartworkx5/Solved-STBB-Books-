Maths Word Problems: Speed of Trains and Bikes

By Solved Sindh Textbook Board Books Team · Updated 2026-11-04 · 1694 words · 8 min read

Speed problems with trains and bikes are exciting because they feel like real races, but they punish students who rush the units or compare the wrong quantities. A train travelling at 60 kilometres per hour and a bike at 15 kilometres per hour cannot be compared fairly unless they travel the same distance or the same time, and many exam questions deliberately mix these cases. The method is always the same: organise distance, speed and time for each vehicle, match the units, and only then compare. This guide works through who-arrives-first problems, unit conversions, and the common traps, with examples you can practise tonight. More help is in our [solved notes](/solved.html) and on the [books page](/books.html).

Speed Is Distance Over Time

Speed tells you how much distance is covered in one unit of time, so speed equals distance divided by time. A train covering 180 kilometres in 3 hours travels at 60 kilometres per hour, because 180 divided by 3 equals 60. A bike covering 30 kilometres in 2 hours travels at 15 kilometres per hour, because 30 divided by 2 equals 15. Comparing these two speeds is easy once both are in the same unit: the train is four times as fast as the bike. The danger comes when one speed is in kilometres per hour and the other in metres per second, because the numbers then look misleading until converted.

Always write the unit beside every speed, distance and time, and convert before comparing. A speed of 20 metres per second sounds slower than 60 kilometres per hour, but converting shows that 20 metres per second equals 72 kilometres per hour, which is actually faster. The conversion rule is simple: multiply metres per second by 3.6 to get kilometres per hour, and divide kilometres per hour by 3.6 to get metres per second. Practise this conversion until it is automatic, because examiners love mixing units in speed questions.

Trains: Who Arrives First

Who-arrives-first problems give two vehicles travelling the same distance at different speeds and ask which arrives first and by how much. Calculate each journey time separately with time equals distance divided by speed, then subtract. For example, two trains travel 240 kilometres: the express at 80 kilometres per hour takes 3 hours, while the local at 60 kilometres per hour takes 4 hours. The express arrives 1 hour earlier. The mistake to avoid is assuming the faster train always wins by a simple margin; the time difference depends on the distance too, so a small speed advantage over a long distance creates a big time gap.

Some questions add different start times, which changes the answer completely. If the slow train leaves one hour before the fast train on the same 240-kilometre route, the slow train arrives after 4 hours while the fast train, leaving an hour later, arrives after 3 hours of travel, which is also 4 hours after the slow train left. In this case they arrive together, even though their speeds differ. To handle start-time problems, convert every arrival into clock time: add each journey time to its own start time, and then compare the clock times. This method never fails, however tricky the question looks.

Bikes and Unit Conversion

Bike problems often mix kilometres, metres, hours, minutes and seconds, because cycling speeds feel natural in both kilometres per hour and metres per second. A cyclist covering 900 metres in 60 seconds travels at 15 metres per second, which converts to 54 kilometres per hour by multiplying by 3.6. If the question gives distance in kilometres and time in minutes, convert the minutes to hours first: 45 minutes is 0.75 hours, so a 18-kilometre ride in 45 minutes means a speed of 18 divided by 0.75, which is 24 kilometres per hour. Writing each conversion as its own line keeps the working clean and checkable.

A favourite exam variation asks how far a bike travels in a given time at a given speed, which uses distance equals speed times time. A bike at 20 kilometres per hour for 30 minutes, which is 0.5 hours, covers 10 kilometres. Another variation asks how long a journey takes at a changed speed: if the same 10-kilometre ride is done at 25 kilometres per hour instead, the time is 10 divided by 25, which is 0.4 hours, or 24 minutes. Notice how 0.4 hours becomes 24 minutes by multiplying by 60, not by reading it as 40 minutes. Decimal-to-minutes conversion is tested again and again.

Overtaking and Meeting Problems

Overtaking problems describe a faster vehicle catching a slower one, and meeting problems describe two vehicles moving towards each other. For meeting problems, the key idea is that their speeds add up, because together they cover the distance between them. If a train at 70 kilometres per hour and another at 50 kilometres per hour move towards each other across 240 kilometres, they close the gap at 120 kilometres per hour, so they meet after 2 hours. The first train has then travelled 140 kilometres and the second 100 kilometres, and 140 plus 100 equals 240, which checks the answer beautifully.

For overtaking problems, the key idea is the opposite: subtract the speeds, because only the speed difference closes the gap. If a bike at 25 kilometres per hour starts 10 kilometres ahead of a bike at 35 kilometres per hour, the gap closes at 10 kilometres per hour, so the faster bike catches up after 1 hour. Students often add speeds in overtaking problems by mistake, which gives an answer that is far too quick. Remember the simple rule: towards each other means add the speeds, same direction means subtract them, and the rest of the method is the same distance-speed-time calculation.

Common Mistakes in Speed Problems

The first common mistake is comparing speeds in different units without converting, which makes a slower vehicle look faster. The second is using minutes directly with kilometres per hour, giving an answer 60 times too large. The third is reading a decimal time like 0.4 hours as 40 minutes instead of 24 minutes. The fourth is adding speeds in an overtaking problem or subtracting them in a meeting problem. The fifth is ignoring different start times in who-arrives-first problems, which can reverse the winner entirely. Each of these mistakes is a reading and conversion error, not a formula error.

To avoid them, always list distance, speed, time and start time for every vehicle in a small table, convert all units before calculating, and finish by converting journey times into clock arrival times. Then sense-check: a bike cannot travel at 200 kilometres per hour, and a train journey of 240 kilometres cannot take 10 minutes. If an answer looks impossible, recheck the conversions first. With this disciplined approach, speed problems become fast, reliable marks. More guides are on our blog and about page.

5 key points you must know

  • Speed equals distance divided by time; always write the unit beside every value.
  • Convert metres per second to kilometres per hour by multiplying by 3.6, and reverse by dividing.
  • Who arrives first: find each journey time, add it to its start time, compare clock times.
  • Meeting vehicles add their speeds; overtaking vehicles subtract their speeds.
  • Convert decimal hours to minutes by multiplying by 60, never by reading the decimal as minutes.

Frequently asked questions

How do I decide who arrives first in a race problem?

Calculate each vehicle's journey time using time equals distance divided by speed, add each journey time to that vehicle's own start time, and compare the resulting clock times. The earlier clock time wins, even if that vehicle is slower but started earlier. Ignoring different start times is the most common error in these problems, so always convert journey times into arrival clock times before comparing.

How do I convert metres per second to kilometres per hour?

Multiply the speed in metres per second by 3.6 to get kilometres per hour, and divide kilometres per hour by 3.6 to get metres per second. For example, 20 metres per second equals 72 kilometres per hour, which surprises many students because the number looks small before conversion. Always convert speeds to the same unit before comparing them, or the faster vehicle may appear slower.

What is the difference between meeting and overtaking problems?

In meeting problems, two vehicles move towards each other, so their speeds add together to close the gap. In overtaking problems, both move in the same direction, so only the difference of their speeds closes the gap. For example, trains at 70 and 50 kilometres per hour meeting head-on close at 120 kilometres per hour, while a bike at 35 catching one at 25 closes at only 10 kilometres per hour.

How do I convert 0.4 hours into minutes correctly?

Multiply the decimal part by 60, because one hour contains 60 minutes. So 0.4 hours equals 0.4 times 60, which is 24 minutes, not 40 minutes. Similarly, 2.5 hours is 2 hours and 30 minutes, and 0.75 hours is 45 minutes. This conversion is tested very often, so practise it until it feels automatic before your exam.

Why do speed problem answers often look impossible?

Impossible answers almost always come from unit errors: using minutes with kilometres per hour, comparing speeds in different units, adding speeds when they should be subtracted, or misreading decimal hours as minutes. Listing every vehicle's distance, speed, time and start time in a table, converting units before calculating, and sense-checking the final answer against real-world speeds will catch nearly all of these mistakes.
