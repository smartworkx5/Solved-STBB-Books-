Maths Word Problems: Area of Rooms and Fields

By Solved Sindh Textbook Board Books Team · Updated 2026-11-04 · 1776 words · 9 min read

Area problems describe real jobs: laying tiles on a floor, putting a fence around a field, or painting the walls of a room. Students often know the area formula for a rectangle but still lose marks, because they confuse area with perimeter, mix up units, or forget to subtract doors and windows when painting. The secret is to draw a quick sketch, label the sides, and decide before calculating whether the question is about covering a surface or going around an edge. This guide works through flooring, fencing and painting problems with clear methods you can reuse in exams. Area and Perimeter Are Not the Same

Area measures the surface covered inside a shape, while perimeter measures the distance around its edge, and confusing the two is the most common error in this topic. For a rectangle, area equals length multiplied by breadth, and perimeter equals twice the sum of length and breadth. A room 5 metres long and 4 metres wide has an area of 20 square metres and a perimeter of 18 metres. Notice the units: area is always in square units, such as square metres, while perimeter is in ordinary units, such as metres. Writing the unit correctly is itself worth marks and also acts as a check on your method.

Before calculating, ask yourself whether the job covers a surface or follows an edge. Flooring, tiling, carpeting and painting are area jobs, because they cover a surface. Fencing, skirting boards and borders are perimeter jobs, because they run around an edge. A question about a fence around a field needs the perimeter, while a question about grass seed for the same field needs the area. Many exam questions deliberately test this distinction by describing a field and asking about fencing, so underline the key verb: fence, surround and border mean perimeter, while cover, tile and paint mean area.

Flooring a Room: Tiles and Carpets

Flooring problems usually give the room dimensions and the tile size, and ask how many tiles are needed. The method is to find the area of the floor and divide it by the area of one tile. For example, a floor 6 metres long and 4 metres wide has an area of 24 square metres. If one tile covers 1 square metre, then 24 tiles are needed. If tiles are smaller, convert carefully: a tile 50 centimetres by 50 centimetres covers 0.25 square metres, so the same floor needs 24 divided by 0.25, which is 96 tiles. Convert all lengths to the same unit before calculating, or the answer will be wrong by a large factor.

Carpet problems are simpler because carpet is usually sold by area, so you only need the floor area, plus sometimes the cost. If carpet costs 300 rupees per square metre and a room measures 5 metres by 4 metres, the area is 20 square metres and the cost is 20 times 300, which is 6000 rupees. A common trap is a room with an attached bathroom or a missing corner, which makes the floor an L-shape rather than a rectangle. Split an L-shape into two rectangles, find each area separately, and add them together. Drawing the split on your sketch makes this step obvious and prevents missing a section.

Fencing a Field: Perimeter Problems

Fencing problems ask for the length of fence needed around a field, which is the perimeter, and sometimes the cost of that fence. For a rectangular field 40 metres long and 30 metres wide, the perimeter is twice the sum of 40 and 30, which is 140 metres. If fencing costs 50 rupees per metre, the total cost is 140 times 50, which is 7000 rupees. Watch for questions that leave a gap for a gate: if a 4-metre gate is left open, subtract it from the perimeter before calculating the cost, giving 136 metres of fence and a cost of 6800 rupees. Reading the gate detail is essential.

Some fencing problems involve several rows of wire, such as a field fenced with 3 rounds of wire. Here you must multiply the perimeter by the number of rounds before finding the cost. A field with a perimeter of 140 metres fenced with 3 rounds needs 420 metres of wire. Another variation gives the perimeter and one side and asks for the other side: if the perimeter is 100 metres and the length is 30 metres, then twice the breadth equals 100 minus 60, which is 40, so the breadth is 20 metres. Working backwards like this is a favourite exam style, so practise it until it feels natural.

Painting Walls: Subtracting Doors and Windows

Painting problems ask for the area to be painted, which is usually the area of the walls minus the area of doors and windows that are not painted. Suppose a wall is 5 metres long and 3 metres high, giving an area of 15 square metres. If the wall has a door 2 metres by 1 metre, the door area is 2 square metres, so the paintable area is 13 square metres. For a room with four walls, calculate each wall separately or use the perimeter times the height, then subtract all doors and windows. Students who forget this subtraction overstate the paint needed and lose the mark.

Paint cost problems add one more step: multiply the paintable area by the cost per square metre, or divide by the coverage of one tin. If one tin of paint covers 10 square metres and the paintable area is 45 square metres, you need 45 divided by 10, which is 4.5 tins, so you must buy 5 tins because paint is sold in whole tins. Always round up when buying materials like tiles or paint tins; rounding down would leave the job unfinished. This practical rounding rule is easy to forget in an exam, but examiners test it deliberately.

Common Mistakes in Area Problems

The first common mistake is confusing area with perimeter, answering a fencing question with an area or a tiling question with a perimeter. The second is mixing units, such as multiplying metres by centimetres without converting, which gives an answer out by a factor of 100. The third is forgetting to subtract doors and windows in painting problems, or forgetting to subtract a gate in fencing problems. The fourth is rounding down when buying tiles or paint tins, leaving the job short. Each of these errors comes from rushing, not from a lack of knowledge.

To avoid them, always start with a sketch, label every side with its unit, and write whether the question needs area or perimeter before calculating. Convert all lengths to the same unit first, show the formula you are using, and finish with the correct square or ordinary unit. After finding the answer, do a sense check: a classroom floor cannot be 2 square metres, and a field fence cannot be 5 metres long. These quick checks catch most errors before you hand in your paper. 5 key points you must know

  • Area covers a surface; perimeter runs around an edge — decide which one first.
  • Rectangle area equals length times breadth; perimeter equals twice length plus breadth.
  • Convert all lengths to the same unit before multiplying, or the answer will be wrong.
  • Painting area means wall area minus doors and windows; fencing means perimeter minus gates.
  • Round up when buying tiles or paint tins; rounding down leaves the job unfinished.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between area and perimeter?

Area measures the surface inside a shape, while perimeter measures the distance around its edge. For a rectangle, area equals length multiplied by breadth and is measured in square units, while perimeter equals twice the sum of length and breadth and is measured in ordinary units. Flooring, tiling and painting are area jobs, while fencing and borders are perimeter jobs, so identifying the job correctly is the first step in every problem.

How do I find how many tiles are needed for a floor?

Find the area of the floor by multiplying its length by its breadth, find the area of one tile the same way, and divide the floor area by the tile area. For example, a floor of 24 square metres covered with tiles of 0.25 square metres needs 96 tiles. Convert all lengths to the same unit first, and round up the final tile count, because you cannot buy part of a tile and the job must be fully covered.

How do I solve a fencing problem with a gate?

First calculate the perimeter of the field, then subtract the width of the gate, because no fence is needed across the opening. For a field with a perimeter of 140 metres and a 4-metre gate, the fence length is 136 metres. If fencing costs a certain amount per metre, multiply the fence length by that rate. If several rounds of wire are used, multiply the perimeter by the number of rounds before finding the cost.

Why do painting problems subtract doors and windows?

Doors and windows are not painted, so their area must be removed from the wall area to find the true paintable area. Calculate each wall's area, add them together, then subtract the area of every door and window. Forgetting this step overstates the paint needed and loses the mark. After finding the paintable area, divide by the coverage of one tin and round up, because paint is sold in whole tins.

What mistakes should I avoid in area problems?

The most common mistakes are confusing area with perimeter, mixing metres and centimetres without converting, forgetting to subtract doors, windows or gates, and rounding down when buying materials. Always sketch the shape, label sides with units, state whether area or perimeter is needed, convert units first, and sense-check the final answer against the real size of the room or field described.
