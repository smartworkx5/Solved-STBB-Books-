Maths Word Problems: Ages of Family Members

By Solved Sindh Textbook Board Books Team · Updated 2026-11-04 · 1876 words · 9 min read

Age problems are a favourite exam topic because they look like stories about families but are really simple equations in disguise. The key idea is beautifully simple: everyone ages at the same speed, so the difference between two people's ages never changes. If a father is 30 years older than his son today, he will still be 30 years older in ten years, and he was 30 years older ten years ago. Once you understand this, problems about now, after five years and five years ago all follow the same method. This guide explains that method with worked examples your whole family can follow. More practice is in our [solved notes](/solved.html) and on the [books page](/books.html).

Ages Move Together: The Golden Rule

The golden rule of age problems is that the difference between two ages stays constant over time. If Ali is 12 and his sister is 8, the difference is 4 years, and it will still be 4 years when Ali is 20 and his sister is 16. This rule lets you check your answers quickly: if your final ages give a different difference from the one in the question, something has gone wrong. It also lets you solve problems backwards, because knowing one person's age and the difference immediately gives you the other person's age at any point in time.

The second idea is that ages shift by the same amount for everyone. After five years, every person is five years older, so you add five to every age in the problem. Five years ago, every person was five years younger, so you subtract five from every age. Students often add five to only one person's age and forget the other, which breaks the golden rule and gives a wrong answer. A safe habit is to write a small table with columns for now, after five years and five years ago, and fill in every row before calculating anything.

Problems About Now

Problems about now usually give a sum of ages or a ratio of ages and ask for each person's age. For example, a father and son have ages in the ratio 5:1, and the sum of their ages is 48 years. The total parts are 6, so one part is worth 8 years, giving the father 40 years and the son 8 years. Check: 40 plus 8 equals 48, and the difference is 32 years. Another common type gives the difference directly: if a mother is 26 years older than her daughter and their ages add up to 50, then twice the daughter's age equals 50 minus 26, which is 24, so the daughter is 12 and the mother is 38.

When a problem about now gives a multiple, such as a father being three times as old as his son, use the same parts method with the multiple as a ratio. If the father is three times as old as his son and their ages sum to 44, the ratio is 3:1, the total parts are 4, and one part is 11 years, so the son is 11 and the father is 33. Always check both the sum and the multiple in your answer. If either check fails, your working has an error, and rechecking the parts division usually finds it. Writing the checks beside your answer also earns method marks in exams.

After Five Years Problems

After-five-years problems describe a relationship that will hold in the future, and the key step is to add five to every age before setting up the equation. For example, a son is 10 years old and his father is 38. After five years, the son will be 15 and the father will be 43. A typical exam question reverses this: a father is now three times as old as his son, but after five years he will be only twice as old. Let the son's age now be x, so the father is 3x. After five years they are x plus 5 and 3x plus 5, and the equation 3x plus 5 equals twice x plus 5 gives x equals 5, so the son is 5 and the father is 15.

The most common error in future problems is adding the years to only one person, which destroys the constant difference and produces an impossible answer. Another error is writing the future relationship using present ages, for example setting the father's present age equal to twice the son's future age. To avoid this, write the future ages in a separate line of your working, clearly labelled after five years, and build the equation only from that line. If your answer gives a negative age or a father younger than his son, you know immediately that a step went wrong.

Five Years Ago Problems

Five-years-ago problems work exactly like future problems but in reverse: subtract five from every age before building the equation. For example, a mother is now 34 and her daughter is 10. Five years ago, the mother was 29 and the daughter was 5. A typical exam question says that five years ago, a father was four times as old as his son, and today the father is 40. Five years ago the father was 35, so the son was 35 divided by 4, which is not a whole number, and that tells you the question must have different numbers; in a well-set question the division works out neatly. Real exam questions are designed to give whole-number answers, so a fraction is a warning sign.

Consider a proper example: five years ago, a father was 5 times as old as his son, and today the father is 45. Five years ago the father was 40, so the son was 40 divided by 5, which is 8 years old then, and 13 years old now. The check is that today the father is 45 and the son is 13, a difference of 32 years, and five years ago the difference was also 32 years, since 40 minus 8 equals 32. This difference check is your best friend in past problems. If the difference changes between your present and past ages, you have added or subtracted the years unevenly.

Common Mistakes in Age Problems

The first common mistake is shifting only one person's age when moving to the future or the past, which breaks the constant age difference. The second is mixing up present and future ages in the same equation, for example using the father's present age with the son's future age. The third is forgetting that ages must be whole numbers and positive; an answer like negative 3 years or a son older than his father is always wrong. The fourth is solving the equation correctly but answering the wrong question, such as giving the son's age when the question asked for the father's age in five years' time.

To avoid these traps, always write a table with rows for each person and columns for now, future and past, and fill it in completely before writing any equation. Solve the equation step by step, showing each line, and finish by checking the sum, the ratio and the constant difference. Finally, reread the question and confirm you answered exactly what was asked, including whether it wanted a present age or a future age. 5 key points you must know

  • The difference between two people's ages never changes over time.
  • After five years, add five to every age; five years ago, subtract five from every age.
  • Use a table with rows for people and columns for now, future and past.
  • Check every answer against the sum, the ratio and the constant age difference.
  • Ages must be positive whole numbers; a negative or fractional age signals an error.

Frequently asked questions

What is the golden rule of age problems?

The golden rule is that the difference between two people's ages never changes, because everyone ages at the same speed. If a father is 30 years older than his son today, he will still be 30 years older in ten years and he was 30 years older ten years ago. This rule gives you a powerful check: if your final ages show a different difference from the one in the question, your working contains an error, usually from shifting only one person's age.

How do I solve an age problem about five years from now?

Add five years to every person's age before building your equation, and use only the future ages in the relationship the question describes. For example, if a father is now three times as old as his son but will be twice as old after five years, write the son's present age as x, the father's as 3x, then set 3x plus 5 equal to twice x plus 5, giving x equals 5. Adding the years to only one person is the most common mistake in these problems.

How do five-years-ago problems work?

Subtract five years from every person's age before setting up the equation, exactly mirroring future problems. If a father is 45 today, he was 40 five years ago, and if he was then five times as old as his son, the son was 8 then and is 13 now. Check your answer with the constant difference: the father-son difference must be the same now as it was five years ago. If it changed, you shifted the ages unevenly.

How do I use ratios in age problems?

Treat the ratio like any sharing ratio: add the ratio numbers for total parts, divide the sum of ages by total parts to find one part, then multiply by each share. If a father and son are in the ratio 5:1 and their ages sum to 48, total parts are 6, one part is 8 years, so the father is 40 and the son is 8. Always verify both the sum and the ratio in your final answer before moving on.

Why do my age problem answers come out impossible?

Impossible answers, such as negative ages, fractional ages or a son older than his father, usually come from shifting only one person's age, mixing present and future ages in one equation, or setting up the equation from the wrong time period. Writing a table for now, future and past, filling every cell before calculating, and checking the constant age difference will catch these errors. Reread the question at the end to confirm you answered the age it actually asked for.
