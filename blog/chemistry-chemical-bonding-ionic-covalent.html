Chemical Bonding: Ionic vs Covalent, Explained Simply

By Solved Sindh Textbook Board Books Team · Updated 2026-10-19 · 1520 words · 8 min read

Atoms rarely live alone. Most atoms join together to form compounds, and the force that holds them together is called a chemical bond. Chemical bonding comes in two main types that every chemistry pupil must master: ionic bonding, where electrons are transferred from one atom to another, and covalent bonding, where electrons are shared between atoms. The reason atoms bond at all is stability: atoms with incomplete outer shells gain, lose, or share electrons to reach a full outer shell, usually of eight electrons. This drive toward a full outer shell explains why sodium and chlorine form a compound, why two hydrogen atoms pair up, and why water holds together. In this guide we compare ionic and covalent bonding side by side, using dot-and-cross diagrams — the standard drawing method that shows exactly which electrons move and which are shared. By the end you will be able to look at a pair of elements, predict the bond type, draw the diagram, and explain the properties of the compound that forms.

Why Atoms Bond: The Full Outer Shell

An atom with a full outer shell is stable, like the noble gases helium, neon, and argon, which almost never react. Other atoms have outer shells that are partly empty, and bonding is how they complete them. A sodium atom has one outer electron; losing it leaves a full shell underneath. A chlorine atom has seven outer electrons; gaining one completes its shell. So sodium and chlorine are a natural pair: one wants to lose an electron, the other wants to gain one, and the compound they form gives both atoms stable arrangements.

The same idea explains sharing. A hydrogen atom has one electron and needs one more to fill its first shell, which holds only two. Two hydrogen atoms can each share their electron with the other, so both feel as if they have two. Sharing works when neither atom is willing to give up its electron completely — a common situation between two non-metals. Whether electrons are transferred or shared depends on which elements are involved, and that is the heart of the ionic-versus-covalent distinction.

Ionic Bonding: Transfer of Electrons

Ionic bonding happens between a metal and a non-metal. The metal atom loses one or more electrons to become a positively charged ion, and the non-metal atom gains those electrons to become a negatively charged ion. The opposite charges then attract each other strongly, and that electrostatic attraction is the ionic bond. Sodium chloride, common table salt, is the classic example: sodium loses its outer electron to become Na+, chlorine gains it to become Cl-, and the ions pack together in a giant crystal lattice.

Ionic compounds have distinctive properties that follow from this structure. They are usually solids with high melting points, because breaking apart a lattice of strongly attracted ions takes a lot of energy. They conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water, because the ions are then free to move, but not as solids, because the ions are locked in place. They are often soluble in water. If an exam asks you to explain any of these properties, always link back to the ions and the lattice — that connection is what earns the marks.

  • Metal loses electrons to form a positive ion; non-metal gains electrons to form a negative ion.
  • The bond is the attraction between opposite charges.
  • Example: Na+ and Cl- in sodium chloride, held in a giant ionic lattice.
  • Properties: high melting point, conducts when molten or dissolved, brittle crystals.

Covalent Bonding: Sharing Electrons

Covalent bonding happens between two non-metals, which share pairs of electrons so that each atom reaches a full outer shell. In a hydrogen molecule, one shared pair gives each atom two electrons. In a water molecule, the oxygen atom shares one pair with each hydrogen atom, giving oxygen eight outer electrons and each hydrogen two. In a methane molecule, carbon shares four pairs with four hydrogen atoms. A single shared pair is a single bond; two shared pairs make a double bond, as in oxygen gas, and three shared pairs make a triple bond, as in nitrogen gas.

Covalent substances have very different properties from ionic ones. Simple covalent molecules, like water or carbon dioxide, usually have low melting and boiling points because only weak forces hold the separate molecules together, even though the bonds inside each molecule are strong. They do not conduct electricity, because there are no free ions or electrons. Giant covalent structures like diamond are the exception: every atom is bonded into one huge network, making diamond extremely hard with a very high melting point. When you describe covalent properties in an exam, always say whether you mean simple molecules or a giant structure.

Dot-and-Cross Diagrams: Drawing Bonds

Dot-and-cross diagrams are the standard way to show bonding in school chemistry. Draw the symbol of each atom, then mark the electrons of one atom as dots and the electrons of the other atom as crosses, so the examiner can see which electron came from where. For ionic bonding, show the transfer with an arrow: the metal's outer electron moves to the non-metal, then draw the two ions in square brackets with their charges. For covalent bonding, draw the shared pairs between the atoms, with each pair containing one dot and one cross where possible.

Work through a few standard examples until the method is automatic: sodium chloride and magnesium oxide for ionic bonding; hydrogen, chlorine, water, methane, and carbon dioxide for covalent bonding. Always check your finished diagram: every atom should end with a full outer shell (two for hydrogen, eight for the rest), and the total number of dots and crosses must match the total electrons of the atoms involved. A diagram that fails either check has an error. For more on careful exam technique, see How to Avoid Common Exam Mistakes.

Predicting Bond Type from the Elements

The fastest way to predict bond type is to look at the two elements. Metal plus non-metal usually means ionic bonding: the metal loses electrons and the non-metal gains them. Non-metal plus non-metal usually means covalent bonding: the atoms share electrons. Two metals bond differently again, through metallic bonding, which you will meet separately. This simple rule answers most school-level questions, and the periodic table helps you decide which elements are metals: they sit on the left and in the centre, while non-metals sit on the right.

When you write an answer, follow a three-step pattern: name the bond type, explain what happens to the electrons, and link to a property. For example: sodium chloride is ionic because sodium transfers its outer electron to chlorine; the oppositely charged ions attract strongly, which is why the compound has a high melting point. That structure — type, electrons, property — works for almost every bonding question. 5 key points you must know

  • Atoms bond to reach a full outer shell of electrons.
  • Ionic bonding = electron transfer between a metal and a non-metal.
  • Covalent bonding = electron sharing between two non-metals.
  • Dot-and-cross diagrams show exactly which electrons move or are shared.
  • Bond type explains properties such as melting point and conductivity.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between ionic and covalent bonding?

In ionic bonding, electrons are transferred from a metal to a non-metal, forming oppositely charged ions that attract. In covalent bonding, electrons are shared between two non-metals so each atom reaches a full outer shell.

Why does sodium chloride have a high melting point?

Sodium chloride is a giant lattice of Na+ and Cl- ions held by strong electrostatic attraction. A lot of energy is needed to break those attractions, so the melting point is high.

Why do simple covalent molecules have low melting points?

The bonds inside each molecule are strong, but only weak forces hold separate molecules together. Little energy is needed to separate the molecules, so melting and boiling points are low.

What does a dot-and-cross diagram show?

It shows the outer electrons of each atom, with dots for one atom's electrons and crosses for the other's, so the transfer or sharing of electrons is visible. Finished atoms should show full outer shells.

Can a metal bond with another metal ionically?

No. Ionic bonding needs a metal that loses electrons and a non-metal that gains them. Two metals form metallic bonding instead, where electrons move freely through the metal structure.
