Urdu Poetry: Rubai and Its Four Lines

By Solved Sindh Textbook Board Books Team · Updated 2026-11-04 · 1674 words · 8 min read

The rubai is one of the shortest and most powerful forms of Urdu poetry, because it says a complete thought in just four lines. Students often meet rubaiyat in lessons and in collections of famous poets, but many do not know the rules that make a rubai different from a couplet or a ghazal. This article explains what a rubai is, how its four lines rhyme, why it is famous through the Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam and the rubaiyat of Iqbal, and how students can read, enjoy and answer questions about this beautiful short form.

What Is a Rubai?

A rubai, also spelled rubaiyat in its plural form, is a poem of exactly four lines that expresses one complete idea, feeling or piece of wisdom. The word rubai comes from the Arabic word for four, and the form has been used for centuries in Persian and Urdu poetry to capture a thought that is too big for one couplet but too short for a long poem. A good rubai feels like a small, polished gem: it begins with a picture or question, develops it in the middle lines, and ends with a surprising or thoughtful conclusion that stays in the reader's mind.

The rubai is famous because it suits deep ideas about life, death, love, fate and human nature, and because its short length makes it easy to memorise and quote. Students like rubaiyat because they can learn one complete poem in minutes, unlike a long nazam that takes much longer to master. Teachers also use rubaiyat to teach poetic structure, since the four-line form shows rhyme, rhythm and meaning in a small and clear package. The Four-Line Pattern

The rhyme pattern of a rubai is simple to learn: the first, second and fourth lines rhyme with each other, while the third line is free and does not need to rhyme. This pattern is often written as AABA, where A stands for the rhyming sound and B stands for the free line, and it gives the rubai both music and freedom. The free third line is important because it lets the poet change direction, ask a question, or add a surprise before the fourth line returns to the rhyme and completes the thought with a strong ending.

The four lines also have roles: the first line opens the picture or idea, the second line develops it, the third line turns or questions it, and the fourth line delivers the conclusion or punchline. This shape is similar to a short story with a beginning, middle, twist and ending, and that is why a rubai feels complete even though it is short. Students who remember the AABA pattern and the four roles can analyse any rubai quickly, and they can also try writing their own rubai by following the same shape. Famous Examples: Khayyam and Iqbal

The most famous rubaiyat in the world belong to Omar Khayyam, a Persian poet and scholar whose four-line poems about life, wine, gardens and fate were translated into English by Edward FitzGerald and became world-famous. One of FitzGerald's best-known translated quatrains begins: A Book of Verses underneath the Bough, A Jug of Wine, a Loaf of Bread, and Thou, and it paints a picture of simple happiness in nature that readers across the world still quote. Khayyam's rubaiyat are loved because they ask big questions about life and death in simple, beautiful images that anyone can understand.

In Urdu poetry, Allama Iqbal also wrote famous rubaiyat that carry his message of selfhood, courage and faith, and students meet his short poems in lessons and collections. Iqbal's rubaiyat differ from Khayyam's because they urge action and hope rather than quiet reflection, which shows how the same four-line form can carry very different messages. Students can also practise the form with a simple original example written for this lesson, such as a four-line verse about a student who studies with patience and finds that hard work opens the door of success. The lesson is clear: a rubai needs only four lines, but those lines must carry one complete and thoughtful idea, and our solved notes show more examples.

Rubai vs Ghazal and Nazam

A rubai differs from a ghazal because a ghazal has many couplets that can each stand alone, while a rubai has only four lines that must work together as one poem. It differs from a nazam because a nazam can be long and follow one subject through many stanzas, while a rubai must finish its whole thought in four lines and cannot wander. It also differs from a single couplet, or sher, because a sher gives one idea in two lines, while a rubai develops an idea across four lines with a turn in the third line and a conclusion in the fourth. These differences are often asked about in exams, so students should learn them clearly.

Another difference is the rhyme rule: a rubai follows the AABA pattern, a ghazal follows the pattern fixed by its matla with radif and qafia, and a nazam follows a looser rhyme that serves its story or argument. Students who can state these three differences in an answer show a strong understanding of poetic forms, and they can support the answer with one example of each form. Keeping a small comparison table in a notebook, with form, length and rhyme pattern, is a smart revision habit that makes form questions easy. More comparisons appear on our blog for class study.

How to Appreciate a Rubai

Appreciating a rubai begins with reading it aloud slowly, because its rhythm and rhyme are part of its beauty, and a silent reading often misses the music. Then the student should ask what the single idea is, how the third line changes direction, and why the fourth line feels like a conclusion, because these three questions unlock almost every rubai. Writing the rubai's plain meaning in one sentence is also helpful, since it forces the student to find the core thought instead of repeating the poem's words. This method works for Khayyam, Iqbal and any other rubai the student meets.

For exam answers, students should state the rubai's plain meaning, name its rhyme pattern as AABA, explain the turn in the third line, and finish with the message or wisdom it gives. Trying to write one original rubai is also excellent practice, because it teaches the form from the inside and makes the rules unforgettable. 5 key points you must know

  • A rubai is a poem of exactly four lines expressing one complete idea or piece of wisdom.
  • Its rhyme pattern is AABA: lines one, two and four rhyme, while line three is free.
  • The third line turns or questions the idea, and the fourth line gives the conclusion.
  • Omar Khayyam's Rubaiyat and Iqbal's rubaiyat are the most famous examples for students.
  • A rubai differs from a ghazal's many couplets and a nazam's long single-subject flow.

Frequently asked questions

What is a rubai in Urdu poetry?

A rubai is a short poem of exactly four lines that expresses one complete idea, feeling or piece of wisdom. Its name comes from the Arabic word for four, and it has been used for centuries in Persian and Urdu poetry. A good rubai opens with a picture, develops it, turns in the third line, and ends with a strong conclusion that stays in the reader's mind.

What is the rhyme pattern of a rubai?

The rhyme pattern of a rubai is AABA, which means the first, second and fourth lines rhyme with each other, while the third line is free and does not need to rhyme. The free third line lets the poet change direction or ask a question before the fourth line returns to the rhyme and completes the thought. This pattern gives the rubai both music and freedom.

Who is Omar Khayyam and why are his rubaiyat famous?

Omar Khayyam was a Persian poet and scholar whose four-line poems about life, gardens, fate and simple happiness became world-famous through Edward FitzGerald's English translation. His rubaiyat are loved because they ask big questions about life and death in simple, beautiful images that anyone can understand, and they remain the best-known example of the rubai form.

How is a rubai different from a ghazal?

A rubai has only four lines that must work together as one complete poem, while a ghazal has many couplets that can each stand alone with its own meaning. A rubai follows the AABA rhyme pattern, while a ghazal follows the pattern fixed by its matla with radif and qafia. A rubai is also much shorter and must finish its whole thought in four lines.

How should I answer a rubai question in an exam?

State the rubai's plain meaning in your own words, name its rhyme pattern as AABA, and explain how the third line turns the idea and the fourth line concludes it. Finish with the message or wisdom the rubai gives, such as a lesson about life, patience or courage. Keep the answer short and clear, since a rubai question rewards precise understanding over long writing.
