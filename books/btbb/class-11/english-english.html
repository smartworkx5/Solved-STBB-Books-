Sample solved questions

Of Studies is a famous essay by Francis Bacon (1561–1626), the English philosopher and statesman who laid the groundwork of the modern scientific method. Bacon explains what studies are for, how different books should be read, and how the right study can cure defects of the mind. Three Uses of Studies Studies serve for delight — their chief use for delight is in privateness and retiring (when a man is alone). Studies serve for ornament — they grace our discourse and conversation. Studies serve for ability — they help in the judgment and disposition (handling) of business; general counsels and the marshalling of affairs come best from learned men. The Right Balance Bacon warns against misuse: to spend too much time in studies is sloth ; to use them too much for ornament is affectation ; and to make judgment wholly by their rules is the humour of a scholar . Studies perfect nature, and are perfected by experience — natural abilities are like natural plants that need pruning by study, while studies give directions too large unless they are bounded in by experience. Crafty men condemn studies, simple men admire them, and wise men use them. We should read not to contradict and confute , nor to believe and take for granted, nor to find talk and discourse, but to weigh and consider . How Books Should Be Read Some books are to be tasted — read only in parts. Others are to be swallowed — read fully, but not curiously (not with deep attention). Some few are to be chewed and digested — read wholly, with diligence and attention. Some books may be read by deputy (through extracts made by others), but only the less important ones; distilled books are like common distilled waters — flashy things.
